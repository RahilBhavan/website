The thread through all fourteenEach era fixed the last one's weak point. Cash earned nothing, so it became bitcoin. Bitcoin needed scale, so came debt. Secured debt nearly bit, so everything after was unsecured. Equity was cheapest at a premium, so they sold stock. Income buyers were untapped, so came preferreds. Check yourself

What did the 2022 Silvergate episode teach, and where do you see it today?

That bitcoin pledged as collateral can force a sale at the worst moment. Every security since is unsecured, and the preferreds have no claim on the coins.

Why sell preferreds when 0% convertibles were available?

Convertibles mature or can be put back, and they depend on a buyer pool of arbitrage funds. Preferreds reach income investors and never mature. Strategy's stated reason was building the "fixed income" side of its plan; the refinancing point is analysis.

Chapter 6

The engine: how selling stock can add bitcoin per share

Here is the puzzle. Every time Strategy sells new shares, existing holders own a smaller slice of the company. So how can selling shares be good for them?

The answer is the premium. If the market prices each share above the bitcoin behind it, Strategy can sell a share, buy more bitcoin than that share claims, and leave every other share with a little more bitcoin. The ratio of the share price to the bitcoin behind each share is called mNAV (multiple of net asset value).

The flywheel, and how it can reverse

Debt and preferreds: borrowing the upside

Preferreds and convertibles work differently. Strategy sells a claim worth a fixed number of dollars and buys bitcoin with the money. On day one, nothing changes for common holders: there is more bitcoin, but also a dollar claim of the same size ahead of them. The gain comes later, and only if bitcoin rises faster than the cost of the money. Strategy calls this amplification. It is the same idea as a mortgage on a house.

The rules in one table

My dat-accretion project (Chapter 28) writes each action's day-one effect on net bitcoin per share. m is the net mNAV; q is a preferred's price divided by its $100 face value.

Check yourself

What does common issuance at 0.9x net mNAV do to net bitcoin per share?

If the proceeds acquire bitcoin at unchanged prices under the issuer’s assumptions, net bitcoin per share declines. Management can still issue shares for liquidity or other purposes. The effect on that one metric does not decide which action is best.

Why did Strategy sell MSTR to buy back STRC in mid-2026?

STRC traded below $100 (q < 1) while MSTR traded above its net bitcoin (m > 1). Selling the expensive security to retire the cheap one adds net bitcoin per share while m > q, and it also supports STRC's price.
