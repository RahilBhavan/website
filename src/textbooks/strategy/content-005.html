The price of each product

Every security is a trade. Strategy gets cash now and gives up something later. The buyer gives cash now and is paid for a specific risk. This chapter prices both sides of every product.

Three numbers do most of the work:

  • Cash raised per $100 of claim: how much money Strategy actually received for each $100 it now owes.
  • Yearly cost on the cash raised: the dividend divided by the cash Strategy actually got. This is the real cost of the money.
  • Break-even bitcoin growth: roughly how fast bitcoin must rise for the trade to help common holders. For a preferred spent on bitcoin, it's about the yearly cost on cash raised.

Chapter 13

2020–2022: the first bets, priced

The early decisions were small by today's standards. Their economics set every rule Strategy follows now.

Cash into bitcoin (2020)

The trade. Strategy swapped $250M of cash, which was earning close to nothing in 2020, for 21,454 BTC at an average of about $11,650.

What it cost. The interest the cash would have earned, and the certainty of having it. In 2022, when bitcoin fell to about $15,500, those coins were worth about $330M, still above cost but far below their 2021 peak. Under the accounting of the time, they were also booked as large losses.

What it gained. At $82,131, those first coins are worth about $1.76B, seven times what was paid.

The buyback signal. At the same time, Strategy offered to buy back up to $250M of its own shares. Holders tendered only $60.5M. In economic terms, most owners declined to sell at up to $140 a share. That was a vote to keep their stake in the company and its new plan, and it left more cash for bitcoin.

Borrowing with convertibles (2020–2021)

The trade. $1.7B borrowed through two convertible issues at an average coupon of about 0.3%.

What it saved. At the 6.125% Strategy paid on its secured notes in 2021, the same $1.7B would have cost about $100M a year in interest. The converts cost about $5M.

What it really cost. The difference was paid in options. If MSTR rose above the conversion prices, holders would receive shares, diluting common holders. If it fell, holders would want their cash back. Strategy effectively sold volatility on its own stock to fund bitcoin. Because MSTR was so volatile, that volatility was worth a lot to buyers.

Borrowing against the coins (2021–2022)

The economics. Secured debt was cheaper than unsecured debt would have been, because lenders had the coins as backup. But pledged coins carry a hidden cost: a price fall can force a sale at the bottom. In 2022 bitcoin went below the reported margin level. Strategy wasn't forced to sell, but it had to manage the risk. It later spent $15.3M to get its coins back from the lien.

The rule it created. Never pledge the bitcoin. That rule has a price: Strategy's unsecured preferreds cost 8–12%, more than secured debt. In return, no lender can ever force a sale.

The 2022 accounting loss

Strategy reported a $1.47B net loss for 2022, mostly from $1.29B of bitcoin impairments. Under the old rules a company wrote bitcoin down when the price fell but couldn't write it back up when it recovered. No cash left the company. The economic lesson is to read Strategy's earnings and its cash separately (Chapter 18).

Check yourself

Why were the 2020–21 converts "cheap" but not free?

The low coupon was paid for with an option. Strategy owed either shares if MSTR rose, or cash back if it fell.

What's the economic cost of the "never pledge bitcoin" rule?

Unsecured money costs more: preferreds at 8–12% instead of secured debt rates. In exchange, no lender can force a sale.

Chapter 14

2024–2025: spending the premium

When the market pays three times net assets for your stock, you sell stock. When that window might close, you build other ways to raise money. That's this era in two sentences.

Selling stock at about 3.4x

The economic catch. Every sale at a premium transfers value from new buyers to old holders. New buyers do it willingly only if they expect the premium to persist. So the strategy works as long as the market believes it will keep working.

$3B of 0% convertibles due 2029

The trade. In Nov 2024 Strategy borrowed $3B at 0% interest, convertible at about $672 a share, roughly 55% above MSTR's price at the time.

What it saved. Interest. At even 6%, $3B costs $180M a year.

How it turned out. MSTR fell to about $151 by October 2026, far below $672. The option is nearly worthless, so the notes behave like 0% debt. Holders can put them in June 2028. Strategy repurchased $1.5B of them in May 2026, leaving $1.5B. In effect, the 2024 decision traded a $180M-a-year interest bill for a large principal question in 2028.

Joining the Nasdaq-100

The economics. Index funds tracking the Nasdaq-100 must hold MSTR. That creates steady passive demand, which supports both the price and the trading volume that ATM sales depend on. The flip side showed up in Jan 2026, when MSCI froze its index share counts for bitcoin-heavy companies. Index decisions directly affect how much stock Strategy can sell.

Fair-value accounting and the minimum tax

The risk. The 15% corporate alternative minimum tax is based on book profit. Under fair-value accounting, every $10,000 rise in bitcoin is about $8.5B of paper profit for Strategy. Taxed at 15%, that could have meant roughly $1.3B of cash tax on gains it never sold. That's an illustration, not a calculated liability.

The outcome. After IRS interim guidance (Notice 2025-49), Strategy said in Oct 2025 it no longer expects to owe the tax on unrealized gains. That removed a potential cash drain the size of a year's dividends.

Launching preferreds below face value

The economics. As Chapter 12 shows, the five launches created about $0.9B more claim than cash raised. Why accept that? Because a brand-new security from a B- rated bitcoin company needed a higher starting yield to win buyers. Selling at a discount raises the buyer's yield without printing a higher rate, which keeps the stated rate (and the long-run cost on any later sales at $100) lower.

Making STRC variable

The economics. A fixed-rate preferred's price drifts with interest rates and credit worries. Once it trades below $100, every new sale creates more claim than cash, so issuance stalls. A variable rate lets management push the price back to $100 and keep selling every day. STRC has $17.5B of unsold ATM capacity for that reason.

The cost. The rate rose from 9% to 12% in a year: about $264M a year more at today's size. That is the price of a security that can always be sold.

Selling in euros

The economics. A new investor pool means more demand and potentially a lower rate than selling a sixth US preferred into the same market. The cost is currency risk. So far it has worked in Strategy's favour: the euro weakened after launch, shrinking STRE's dollar claim by about $22M.
