How a stablecoin is made and destroyed

Leave with

  • A mint creates tokens against dollars. A redemption destroys tokens and returns dollars.
  • Only approved parties deal with the issuer. Everyone else trades on markets.
  • Because the price stays near $1, the information is in the quantity, so we count mints and redemptions.

The promise

A stablecoin is a token that a company promises to swap for one US dollar. The company is the issuer. It holds reserves (cash and short-term government debt) worth at least as much as the tokens it has put into circulation.

Three words carry most of the field. Mint means the issuer creates new tokens. Redeem means a holder hands tokens back and gets dollars. Burn is the on-chain name for destroying the tokens that were handed back. A redemption on the books is a burn on the chain.

Two markets

The primary market is the issuer's own window. Only approved customers use it (they pass identity checks and often meet a minimum size). They pay $1.00 and receive one token, or return one token and receive $1.00. The secondary market is any exchange where holders sell tokens to each other. You and I almost always buy there.

Why the price stays near $1

Suppose the token trades at $0.998 on an exchange. An approved buyer can buy it there for $0.998 and redeem it with the issuer for $1.00. That earns $0.002 per token, so buyers keep doing it until the price rises. The same logic works in reverse above $1.00. This is arbitrage, and it is the force that pins the price.

The pin is only as strong as the window. If the issuer pauses redemptions, if banks are closed, or if the buyer doubts the reserves, the arbitrage stops and the price can fall below $1. That is a depeg. Chapter 7 covers one.

Why we count quantities

For a stock, the price changes when beliefs change. For a stablecoin the price barely moves, so price carries little information. What moves is the amount in circulation. People who want more dollar-like tokens cause mints. People who want out cause redemptions. So the useful data are the weekly mints and redemptions. This is the whole idea of the tracker.

Two measures follow, and you must never mix them up:

  • Gross flow = mints + redemptions. It tells you how busy the window is.
  • Net issuance = mints − redemptions. It tells you whether the amount in circulation grew.

Why anyone mints or redeems

People mint to trade other crypto assets, to move dollars across borders, to pay, or to post collateral. They redeem to cash out or to reduce risk. This book makes no claim about which reason dominates in the data. That is something the logs cannot tell you.

Limit

The tracker sees tokens created and destroyed. It does not see who the buyer is or why. A mint by a market maker and a mint by a corporate treasurer look the same on the chain.

Check yourself

Why is a redemption a "burn" on the chain?

The tokens a holder returns are destroyed so that the number in circulation matches the dollars in reserve. The issuer logs a Burn event instead of keeping the tokens.

A token trades at $0.995. Who profits, and what does that do to the price?

An approved buyer who can redeem at $1.00. Buying at $0.995 and redeeming at $1.00 earns $0.005 per token. The buying pushes the price up toward $1.

Why count quantities and not prices for a stablecoin?

The price is held near $1 by arbitrage, so it hides what people want. Mints and redemptions show demand directly.

A market maker mints $100M and burns $100M the same week. What are gross flow and net issuance?

Gross is $200M. Net is $0. Nothing new was added to circulation.

The five issuers and six assets

Leave with

  • Issuers earn the interest on reserves. Holders of a payment stablecoin earn nothing.
  • The study covers five issuers (Circle, Tether, Paxos for PayPal, BlackRock, Ondo) and six assets.
  • Payment stablecoins and Treasury funds are different products. A rate cut should not affect them the same way.

How an issuer makes money

The issuer takes in $100 and puts it into reserves, mostly short-term US Treasury bills and cash-like holdings. Those reserves earn interest. The issuer pays the token holder none of it. The difference is the issuer's income. At a 4% yield, $100 of reserves earns $4.00 a year.

Two consequences matter for this project. First, issuer income moves with interest rates, so a rate cut hurts the issuer even if nobody changes behavior. Second, a holder who wants to earn the yield has to hold something else, such as a Treasury fund. That is why we place payment stablecoins and Treasury-backed products side by side.
