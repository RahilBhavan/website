Governance and adversarial design

After this chapter: Identify effective control rather than relying on a voting label.

Watch for this mistake: Token voting proves that no administrator can override holders.

Governance is an allocation of power, not a promise of decentralization. Map proposal rights, voting eligibility, delegation, quorum, counting rules, execution delays, emergency powers and upgrade keys. Record who can mint, freeze, seize, pause, change fees or replace the logic. A timelock creates a response window only if users can observe and act within it.

Analyze incentives to attack. Borrowed voting power, concentrated delegates, hidden affiliations and low participation can change effective control. A token count alone misses vetoes and administrator authority. A multisignature arrangement can distribute signing authority while still depending on closely related people or one custodian.

Use a stress sheet: halve revenue; reduce price 70%; triple withdrawals; remove subsidies; disable an oracle; lose one signer; let insiders unlock together. Trace effects on cash, supply, security and control. Do not combine assumptions inconsistently: a lower token price may affect both reward expense and collateral.

Worked example: a treasury has $4 million cash, expenses of $500,000 monthly and no revenue. Simple runway is eight months. If half the treasury's reported $4 million is its own thinly traded token, eight months is not a cash-runway conclusion. Model achievable sales, restrictions and expenses explicitly.

Fresh check T6: a DAO advertises community control, but one administrator can upgrade the contract instantly and bypass token votes. Identify effective authority, evidence required and one design improvement with its tradeoff.

The token-demand test

A token can be useful without being scarce relative to demand. Separate a reason to transact from a reason to hold a balance. A customer who buys $10 of tokens and immediately spends them creates a different inventory requirement from a validator who must bond $10,000 for a year.

Suppose customers spend $1 million per month in service tokens. Under a deliberately simple steady-flow model, average working balances equal spending rate times average holding time. If users hold tokens for 0.1 month, balances supporting those payments are $100,000. If they hold for a month, balances are $1 million. This is a working-capital identity under those assumptions, not a valuation model for the entire token. It excludes speculative holdings, security bonds, reserves and changes in behavior.

Ask whether the requirement creates productive coordination or merely imposes a toll. If users can pay stablecoins while an intermediary swaps in the background, service growth may create much less persistent token inventory than the marketing implies. Conversely, collateral requirements may create long-lived holdings but expose users to capital costs and liquidation risk.

Try it: a service processes $600,000 monthly and users hold a five-day balance. Assume a 30-day month. How much working inventory supports the flow? What changes if settlement becomes ten times faster? Explain why neither answer is a price target.

Mechanism design starts with the cheapest deviation

An incentive-compatible mechanism makes the desired action a participant's best response under stated assumptions. Saying “participants are rewarded” is insufficient. A reward may attract fake users, self-dealing or low-quality work rather than useful output.

Imagine an oracle paying reporters for agreeing with the majority. Agreement may reward truth when independent informed reporters dominate. It can reward copying when others' answers are visible. It can reward coordinated falsehood when a coalition controls the majority. The observable metric, timing and cost of deviation determine the mechanism.

Analyze a storage bond with a one-period model. Cheating saves an operator $100. Detection probability is p and the penalty is L. Ignoring all other payoffs, deterrence requires p × L to exceed $100. With 10% detection, a $200 bond gives only $20 expected penalty. A $2,000 penalty gives $200, but only if it is collectible and the operator cannot evade it. Limited liability and false accusations matter.

Try it: detection probability is 25%, cheating saves $300 and the collectible penalty is $800. Is cheating deterred under this model? Name two assumptions that could reverse your conclusion. A good design investigation studies the cheapest attack, not the most dramatic attack.

Emissions are a budget, not a business model

Liquidity mining pays participants to place capital in a system. It can help a new market reach useful depth. It can also attract capital that has no reason to stay when rewards end. Measure retained, useful activity after a reduction in subsidy.

Use a cohort: identify users who arrived during an incentive campaign, distinguish genuine customers from related wallets, and measure subsequent usage. Address-level retention is not necessarily person-level retention. Compare fee revenue from those customers with acquisition subsidy and ongoing servicing cost. A token reward valued at its current market price is a scenario valuation; large sales can realize less.

In a fictional campaign, $200,000 of rewards attracts $10 million of deposits for one month. Customers generate $15,000 in net service revenue before the campaign expense. That month is not profitable merely because deposits grew. If deposits leave afterward, the campaign may have bought temporary liquidity. If some users stay and generate recurring margins, estimate their contribution using observed retention rather than assuming a perpetual flow.

Try it: write a measurement plan that distinguishes organic customer demand, rented liquidity and wash activity. Define a result that would make you discontinue the campaign.

Stablecoins, deposits and fund shares are different claims

A stablecoin aims to maintain a reference value. The stabilizing mechanism may use redeemable reserves, overcollateralized borrowing or other rules. The word “stable” describes an objective, not a guarantee. Identify the actual issuer, holder rights and redemption route.

A bank deposit is a claim on a bank under its legal terms. A fund share is an interest in a pooled investment vehicle. A reserve-backed payment token can have different rights, intermediaries and eligibility. Two instruments that display $1 may react differently to weekends, bank failure, market losses or sanctions controls. Do not infer insurance, priority or direct access to redemption from a quoted price.

A simplified arbitrage model helps explain a peg. If an eligible dealer can acquire a token for $0.98 and redeem for $1 with $0.005 total cost, the apparent margin is $0.015. The trade still requires execution, eligibility, funding, time and confidence that redemption completes. If redemption closes or reserves are uncertain, the displayed discount can persist.

Try it: draw the distinct balance sheets for a token issuer and its reserve custodian. If the custodian holds $10 million for the issuer, does each token holder automatically have a direct claim against the custodian? State the evidence needed instead of guessing.

Collateral creates a threshold, not a safety guarantee

Loan-to-value (LTV) equals debt divided by collateral value. If collateral worth $150 supports $100 debt, LTV is 66.67%. If collateral falls to $120, LTV becomes 83.33% even though debt is unchanged. Liquidation rules can require action before collateral falls below debt.

For a fictional liquidation threshold of 80%, $100 debt reaches the threshold at $125 collateral value. Below that, the design may permit liquidation. A threshold is a trigger, not a promise that assets will sell at the quoted value. Market gaps, fees, delayed prices and thin liquidity can produce a shortfall.

A common reflexive structure uses its own token as collateral for obligations denominated in dollars. A falling token price then reduces backing while weakening confidence. Forced sales can push price down further. Evaluate correlated stress: collateral price, market depth and borrower ability to add collateral may all deteriorate together.

Try it: debt is $6 million, collateral $10 million and the threshold is 75%. Find the collateral value at the trigger and the percentage fall from today's value. What happens if the first executable sale price is below the trigger price?
