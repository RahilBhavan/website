Why we can't just take log(V)

The textbook trick for skewed positive variables is $\log(V)$. But $\log(0)$ is undefined, and 40% of our observations are exactly zero. A common ad-hoc fix is $\log(V + 1)$ — but for small values of $V$, that shift is enormous in relative terms ($\log(0+1) = 0$ vs. $\log(0.1+1) \approx 0.095$ — a tenfold difference in $V$ maps to almost nothing).

The cleaner alternative is the inverse hyperbolic sine transform:

$$\text{IHS}(V) = \log\left(V + \sqrt{V^2 + 1}\right)$$

It is defined at zero ($\text{IHS}(0) = 0$). It behaves approximately like $V$ for small $V$ and like $\log(2V)$ for large $V$, so the marginal interpretation is almost the same as a log transform for the values that matter, but the function handles zeros smoothly. It is the standard transform in labor and household-finance empirical work when an outcome is mostly positive with mass at zero (Pence 2006, Burbidge-Magee-Robb 1988 are the canonical references).

The interpretation of IHS coefficients

The interpretation of an IHS coefficient depends critically on the regime of $V$ in which the change happens. For $V \gg 1$, IHS behaves like $\log(2V)$, so a unit change in $\text{IHS}(V)$ corresponds approximately to a unit change in $\log V$ — and one can read off semi-elasticities the same way as with a log transform. For $V \ll 1$, IHS is approximately linear (IHS$(V) \approx V$), so a unit change in IHS corresponds to a unit change in $V$ itself, and the $e^\beta - 1$ semi-elasticity does not apply.

For our project this distinction matters because most observations live in the $V \in [0, 0.5]$ regime where IHS is closer to linear than to log. So when we report "the CS estimator finds an ATT of 0.42 on IHS$(V)$", the cleanest reading is "AMM listing raises the IHS-transformed velocity by 0.42 units" — which, near $V=1$, is roughly a 42% relative increase, but for tokens like BUIDL (with $\bar V \approx 0.005$) it means an absolute velocity increase of roughly 0.4. We report both readings in the paper's results section and avoid a single misleading "X% increase" headline that does not apply uniformly across the sample.

Winsorization at the 99th percentile

Even after IHS, the top of the distribution has a few extreme observations — weeks where USDY's velocity touched 50+, or where a single large arbitrage event spiked OUSG temporarily. These observations matter, but they also have enormous influence on regression coefficients. The blueprint specifies winsorizing at the 99th percentile — i.e., replacing any value above the 99th percentile with the 99th percentile value itself. We construct both ihs_velocity (unwinsorized) and ihs_velocity_w (winsorized) and report the winsorized version as the main spec, with the unwinsorized as a robustness check (Chapter 14).

Why winsorize at 99% and not 95%? Because at 95% we lose dozens of observations across the panel, and several of those observations are exactly the kind of high-velocity weeks the AMM-listing hypothesis predicts. The 99th percentile cuts only the most extreme single-week spikes (mostly arbitrage events around macro announcements) without truncating the bulk of treatment-relevant variation.

The zero-inflation problem

Even after IHS and winsorization, 40% of our observations are exactly zero. This creates a particular kind of statistical problem: the data-generating process for "any trade happens" may be quite different from the data-generating process for "how much trades, conditional on at least one trade happening." The two processes might respond to AMM listing differently.

A standard fix is a two-part (hurdle) model:

  1. Part 1: a Probit (or logit) for the binary indicator $\mathbb{1}[V_{i,t} > 0]$ — "did any secondary trade happen this week?"
  2. Part 2: an OLS (or PPML) for $\text{IHS}(V_{i,t})$ conditional on $V_{i,t} > 0$ — "given that some trade happened, how much was it?"

The full marginal effect of AMM listing is then the combination of the two pieces: AMM listing might make trading happen at all in some weeks (extensive margin) and also make existing trading weeks bigger (intensive margin). Reporting both separately gives the reader a richer mechanism understanding than a single coefficient ever could.

We do not run two-part as the main spec, for two reasons. First, it complicates the CS estimator (the CS framework is built around continuous outcomes; running it separately on the Probit DV and then on the conditional OLS DV produces two separate ATT objects that have to be combined carefully). Second, with only ~800 observations and ~40% zeros, the Probit half has only ~480 "trade happened" observations to estimate intensive-margin variation. Statistical power on the intensive-margin CS estimate becomes uncomfortably low. We report two-part as a robustness check in Chapter 14 and discuss the mechanism implications.

Back-of-envelope rule for reading IHS coefficients

When you eventually read Table 3 of the paper and see "the ATT is 0.42 on IHS velocity", use the right rule for the value regime you care about:

$$\Delta V / V \approx \Delta \text{IHS}(V) \quad \text{when } V \gg 1 \qquad \text{(semi-elasticity regime)}$$

$$\Delta V \approx \Delta \text{IHS}(V) \quad \text{when } V \ll 1 \qquad \text{(linear regime)}$$

For our project, most token-weeks sit in or near the linear regime, so the cleaner reading of an ATT of 0.42 is "secondary velocity is roughly 0.4 units higher than it would have been in the counterfactual" — not "velocity increased by 52%". Table captions in the paper flag the regime they're translating from.

Chapter 06 · Part III — Causal Inference

The Mafrur paper (arXiv 2508.11651)

A detailed read of the descriptive study this project extends — what it found, what it skipped, and the precise gap a causal estimator can fill.

What Mafrur did

Rifqi Mafrur's working paper RWA Liquidity: A Cross-Asset Empirical Examination of Tokenized Real-World Assets on Public Blockchains (arXiv 2508.11651, August 2025) is the most-cited recent empirical descriptive study of the RWA tokenization sector. It does three things:

  1. Constructs a panel of roughly two dozen tokenized assets — Treasury funds, tokenized real estate, tokenized private credit — across multiple chains, sourcing on-chain transfer data from a mix of public APIs.
  2. Reports summary statistics on holder concentration, transfer frequency, average transaction size, and turnover ratios. Mafrur introduces a clean "liquidity profile" classification — tokens are sorted into "dormant", "intermittent", and "active" buckets based on the fraction of weeks with non-zero secondary activity.
  3. Documents qualitative correlations — for example, that tokens with broader retail KYC tend to have higher turnover, and that tokens listed on AMMs tend to fall in the "active" category. The paper is careful to call these correlations, not causal claims.

What's good about Mafrur

Three things deserve credit. First, the data work is genuinely careful — Mafrur manually curates issuer wallet exclusion lists for every token in the sample, and documents the methodology in a reproducible-looking appendix. Second, the classification scheme is useful: "dormant / intermittent / active" maps nicely onto the way DeFi practitioners describe RWA tokens informally, and it gives a clean way to summarize 24 tokens worth of activity in one table. Third, the paper is aware of its descriptive limits and explicitly punts the causal question — "future work should consider quasi-experimental designs to isolate the effect of AMM listing on secondary market activity" — as the closing line of the conclusion.

That closing line is the explicit invitation this project responds to.

What's missing

Three gaps, in increasing order of how much they matter.

1. No regression of any kind. Mafrur's strongest statement about AMM listing is a cross-tabulation: "of the 11 tokens with active AMM presence, 8 fall in the active liquidity bucket; of the 13 tokens without AMM presence, only 2 do." This is a 2×2 table, not an inferential statement. There is no standard error, no test of the cross-tabulation's significance, no control for token-level heterogeneity. A reader cannot judge whether the difference reflects causal effect, reverse causation (AMMs list tokens that are already active), or selection (issuers who put their tokens on AMMs are systematically different from issuers who don't).

2. No time dimension within token. Mafrur's classification collapses each token's full history into a single category. This throws away the single most useful piece of variation: within-token, before-vs-after AMM listing. For OUSG, which was un-listed for 44 weeks and then listed for 126, Mafrur reports one category ("active") covering both periods. The whole identification opportunity — same token, same issuer, same institutional structure, with a sharp treatment in the middle — is wasted.

3. No counterfactual. Even granting that AMM-listed tokens have higher activity, the question Mafrur cannot answer is: would this token have been more active anyway? The same Ondo growth trajectory that produced OUSG's AMM listing in late 2023 might independently have produced higher velocity. Mafrur's classification cannot distinguish this from a treatment effect. A panel design with token fixed effects, time fixed effects, and a credible parallel-trends assumption can. That is the project this textbook walks you through.
