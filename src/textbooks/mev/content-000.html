Chapter 0

The study on one page

Why you need thisEverything else in this book supports the five sentences below. Read them first, then use the chapters to make each one yours.

  1. In one week (5–11 October 2026) Uniswap v3's WETH/USDT 0.01% pool had 70,669 swaps and 236 sandwich attacks on 244 traders.
  2. The victims lost $12,387 compared with an exact replay of the same block without the attacker.
  3. Where the victim's minimum output could be read, attackers took 98% of the slippage the victim allowed.
  4. Under Ke Wu's per-block two-phase mechanism (Chan, Wu & Shi, AFT 2025), no attack makes money in any run.
  5. With the same orders replayed, victims keep 75% of their loss on average (52%–99% across variants), and about 100% if attackers stop because the attack no longer pays.

Chapter 1

Blocks, ordering and who sees your trade

Why you need thisA sandwich only works because someone controls the order of transactions inside a block. You need to explain who that is.

A transaction is a signed instruction, for example "swap 20,000 USDC for ETH, and revert if I get less than 8.8 ETH". Ethereum groups transactions into a block every 12 seconds. Inside a block the transactions run one after another, so their order decides what price each swap gets.

Today the order is chosen by a builder. Builders compete to give the block proposer (a validator) the most valuable block. Searchers are bots that watch pending transactions and send the builder a bundle: their own transactions in a fixed order, plus a tip. The tip can be a priority fee or a direct ETH transfer to the builder. Profit that comes from choosing, inserting or reordering transactions is called MEV (maximal extractable value). A sandwich is one kind of MEV.

A transaction sent to the public mempool is visible to every searcher before it is mined. A transaction sent through a private RPC goes only to selected builders. Private flow is one reason some big pools see few sandwiches. The fee tier is another: on 7 October the 0.01% USDC/WETH and WETH/USDT pools were each sandwiched about 40 times, while their 0.05% pools were sandwiched 2–3 times.

In this study: 236 sandwiches, mostly built by Titan, BuilderNet, Quasar and Eureka. The top attacker, jaredfromsubway.eth, ran 96 of them. Attackers paid at least $4,400 in fees, 50% of what they took from the pool. Etherscan also shows direct ETH tips to builders, which receipts do not show.

Say it in one breathEthereum runs a block's transactions in order, and builders choose that order, so whoever pays the builder can put trades on both sides of yours.

Check yourself

  1. Why can the attacker be sure its back-run comes right after the victim?

    It sends the front-run, the victim's transaction and the back-run to the builder as one bundle with a fixed order. The builder includes the bundle as a unit or not at all.

  2. Why might a big pool show almost no sandwiches?

    Much of its order flow is private, and a higher fee tier makes both attack legs more expensive. The USDC/WETH 0.05% pool had 13 sandwiches in the week; the 0.01% pools of the same pairs had about 40 a day.

  3. What does an attacker's gas figure miss?

    Direct ETH transfers to the builder inside the transaction (for example 0.251 ETH to Titan Builder in block 26,164,108). Those need traces, so the study's gas number is a lower bound on what attackers paid.

Chapter 2

Automated market makers

Why you need thisThe price impact of a trade is what the attacker exploits. You need to compute it in your head for a simple pool.

An automated market maker (AMM) is a contract that holds two tokens and trades against anyone. The simplest rule is constant product: if the pool holds x ETH and y USDC, every trade must keep x × y = k.

The price is the slope of the curve, y ÷ x USDC per ETH. A buy of ETH adds USDC and removes ETH, so the price rises during the trade. The buyer pays an average price above the starting price. The gap is the price impact, or slippage. A fee (0.01% on the study pool) is charged on the input.

Two prices matter in this study. The average price is what one trade paid overall. The marginal price is the price of the next tiny unit, the slope at the end of the trade. Uniswap users protect themselves with an average price (a minimum output). Ke Wu's mechanism uses a marginal price. Chapter 8 shows why that difference matters.

Say it in one breathIn a constant-product pool your own trade moves the price against you, so anyone who trades in front of you in the same direction makes your price worse.

Check yourself

  1. In the toy pool, what does 20,000 USDC buy, and why not 10 ETH?

    9.0909 ETH. Each ETH removed makes the next one more expensive: the price climbs from 2,000 to 2,420 during the trade.

  2. What is the difference between average and marginal price?

    Average: total paid divided by total received for one trade. Marginal: the price of the next unit at a given pool state, the slope of the curve.

  3. Why is the price impact larger in a smaller pool?

    The same trade is a bigger fraction of the reserves, so the product rule forces a larger price move.
