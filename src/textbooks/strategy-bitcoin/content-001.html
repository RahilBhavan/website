10. Preferred stock: exposure without the same payoff as common

Preferred equity usually trades some participation in common-stock upside for specified dividend and liquidation rights. Perpetual means there is no ordinary fixed maturity. It does not mean the holder can demand principal back whenever desired.

Cumulative means unpaid dividends accumulate according to the terms. That does not guarantee timely cash payment. Non-cumulative means an undeclared period’s dividend does not become an ordinary accumulated dividend entitlement. Convertible adds a route into common shares. Callable means the issuer may redeem under specified conditions.

Use this map as an introduction, then read the linked terms. Stated rates are annual contractual base rates, not current market yields or promised total returns. Payment timing and terms can change through valid amendments.

11. Why a large dividend is not automatically a large return

Illustration C: a preferred share has a $100 stated amount and a 10% annual dividend rate. The scheduled annual cash amount is $10 if paid in full.

  • At an $80 purchase price, simple current yield is $10 ÷ $80 = 12.5%.
  • At a $125 purchase price, it is $10 ÷ $125 = 8%.
  • Buy at $100, receive $10, then sell at $75: total return is −15%, ignoring taxes and fees.

The same contractual rate can coexist with different yields and negative total returns. “Effective yield” on a dashboard may have a specific definition; check it before comparing products.

For a perpetual fixed payment, higher market required yields generally imply lower prices, all else equal. Credit concerns, issuer calls, conversion options, liquidity, and skipped payments complicate the relationship. A target trading price near $100 does not create an unconditional redemption right at $100. The STRC supplement describes maintaining the price near its stated amount as an intention subject to risk. 12. Preferred issuance can increase gross exposure without creating net value at issuance

Illustration D: start with $10,000 of Bitcoin assets, 100 common shares, and no senior claims. Issue $1,000 of non-convertible preferred stock at its $1,000 notional amount and buy $1,000 of Bitcoin.

Gross assets per common share rise from $100 to $110. Subtract the new $1,000 preferred claim and simplified net assets attributable to common remain $10,000, or $100 per share. Future preferred dividends add an economic burden.

If the issuer receives only $900 while creating a $1,000 preferred claim, simplified net assets fall to $9,900 before fees and dividends. Future Bitcoin appreciation might outweigh these costs. That outcome depends on the future; it is not immediate value created by raising money.

This example explains why gross Bitcoin-per-share growth alone is insufficient. It is a balance-sheet illustration, not a full valuation of a perpetual preferred instrument.

Gross BTC per share divides holdings by a specified share count. Strategy uses Assumed Diluted Shares Outstanding for its gross metric: it includes assumed conversions and equity awards under its published method. Its BTC Yield measures change in that ratio. This is not a cash distribution, Bitcoin staking return, or shareholder total return. Strategy metric definitions

Gross BPS = BTC held ÷ assumed diluted shares

Change in gross BPS = ending BPS ÷ starting BPS − 1

14. Net exposure: subtract senior claims consistently

Illustration F: $10,000 of Bitcoin, $500 cash, $2,000 debt, and $1,000 preferred claims leave a simplified $7,500 attributable to 100 common shares: $75 per share. Gross Bitcoin assets are $100 per share. Both calculations can be correct while answering different questions.

For the illustrations in this guide:

Simplified net assets = Bitcoin value + cash − debt − preferred claims

Simplified net assets per share = simplified net assets ÷ common shares

This is a teaching proxy. It excludes the software business, taxes, other assets and liabilities, costs, actual liquidation preferences, and conversion mechanics. It is not GAAP book value or recoverable liquidation value.

Strategy’s current Net BPS uses a more specific treatment: in-the-money convertibles enter the diluted share count, while specified out-of-the-money claims are deducted. Its current mNAV divides common-share price by Net BPS in dollars. Do not deduct a convertible as a full cash claim and also count all its conversion shares without explaining why; that can double-count its burden. July 2026 metric presentation, slide 3

The practical lesson: before comparing two mNAV numbers, write their numerators, denominators, dates, share counts, cash treatment, and claim treatment. A ratio labeled “NAV” is not automatically comparable with another website’s ratio.

15. A premium can erase gains in the underlying assets

A premium means the market price exceeds the selected reference value. A multiple of 2× is a 100% premium, not a 200% premium.

Illustration G: simplified net assets are $100 per share and the market pays 2×, so the share price is $200. Net assets subsequently rise 20% to $120. If the market now pays 1×, the price is $120: a 40% stock-price loss while net assets increased.

For an illustrative fixed-definition decomposition, before dividends:

Ending price ÷ starting price = (ending net value per share ÷ starting net value per share) × (ending multiple ÷ starting multiple)

That is arithmetic, not a predictive model. The market may pay a premium for anticipated future financing, access, liquidity, management, or speculation. It can revise those expectations quickly.

VanEck’s article provides a useful account of the premium/financing relationship. It was published May 22, 2025, uses older observations and forecasts, and discloses positions in Bitcoin, MSTR, STRK, and STRF. Its favorable conclusion is an analyst opinion, not independent proof or a current price target. VanEck analysis and disclosures

16. Leverage changes the residual claim in both directions

Illustration H — fixed senior claims; no conversion, issuance, cash flows, taxes, or software value. Start with $100 of Bitcoin assets and $30 of senior claims. The residual is $70.

The table shows a simplified asset residual, not MSTR’s traded share price or a bankruptcy forecast. Common shareholders have limited liability; a negative accounting residual does not require them to contribute personal cash. Actual recovery and survival depend on the complete balance sheet, timing, law, contracts, and future actions.

Why a fixed “2× Bitcoin” label misleads: the residual changes relative to gross assets as Bitcoin moves. Financing, conversions, and the premium also change. MSTR has no contractual promise to deliver a fixed multiple of Bitcoin’s return.

17. Solvency, liquidity, and returns are different tests

Solvency test: do assets support the claims, under realistic valuations and legal priorities?

Liquidity test: can the company obtain the cash needed when payments fall due?

Shareholder-return test: did the investor’s security produce a satisfactory total return from the price paid?

A company can survive while common shareholders lose most of their investment. It can have substantial assets and still face a difficult cash deadline. Avoid using one of these tests as proof of another.

Build a cash bridge for the period being studied:

Starting usable cash + operating cash + financing + asset-sale proceeds − dividends − interest − repayments − other cash uses = ending cash

Illustration I: a $1,000 cash reserve with $200 annual interest/dividend payments suggests five years of that narrow expense coverage. Add $800 of principal due next year and only $200 remains for other uses. A coverage ratio that omits principal is not a full liquidity forecast.

Bitcoin does not generate the cash to pay a preferred dividend merely by sitting in custody. Cash can come from operations, new financing, reserves, or selling assets. Lending or other asset deployment introduces additional counterparties and risk. Identify the actual source, rather than describing all distributions as income earned by the Bitcoin.

18. A practical downside investigation

For each case, ask both “can the company meet payments?” and “what happens to my security?”

  1. Bitcoin falls: assets shrink; senior claims become larger relative to them. What needs cash before recovery?
  2. The premium contracts: common issuance may become less attractive. Does the company stop expanding, issue anyway, sell assets, or repurchase securities?
  3. Markets close temporarily: test the reserve against actual payment dates, not a perpetual-refinancing assumption.
  4. Required yields rise: preferred prices can fall and new funding can become more expensive.
  5. Dividends are interrupted: distinguish accumulating claims from skipped non-cumulative payments. Examine escalation and voting provisions.
  6. Conversion is unattractive: debt may remain debt; do not assume a rally will remove repayment needs.
  7. Custody or legal trouble occurs: who holds the assets, what controls exist, and what recourse does your security provide?
  8. The software business weakens: separate operating resilience from treasury market gains.

These are investigation prompts grounded in the issuer’s risk disclosures, not claims that each event will occur. Strategy offering risk summary

Sequence matters: a recovery after a cash deadline may arrive too late to avoid an unfavorable financing or sale. A forecast of eventual appreciation does not solve interim cash requirements.
