RWA Velocity & Causal Inference

How does putting a tokenized U.S. Treasury on a Uniswap pool change how often it actually trades? And how do you prove the answer with seven tokens and a noisy on-chain panel?

This textbook teaches the full stack of ideas behind one specific empirical paper: a staggered difference-in-differences estimate of AMM listing's causal effect on secondary-market velocity across seven tokenized U.S. Treasury instruments (BUIDL, OUSG, USDY, BENJI, USTB, USYC, STBT).

We start from first principles. By the end you will be able to: read a raw ERC-20 Transfer event log; explain why two-way fixed effects under staggered treatment embeds forbidden 2×2 comparisons that subtract dynamic treatment effects in the wrong direction; sketch the Callaway-Sant'Anna estimator on a whiteboard; defend your choice of never-treated vs. not-yet-treated control groups; and articulate exactly why the project's N=7 sample is both its greatest asset and its most brutal constraint.

How to use this textbook

Read it linearly if you're new to either econometrics or DeFi — the chapters build on each other. Skip around if you already know parts. Every chapter has at least one interactive component: drag a slider to shift treatment timing, click into a Bacon decomposition to see which 2×2 comparisons are doing the forbidden work, simulate pre-trend tests under varying effect sizes.

What this textbook is not

It is not a replacement for the original sources. The Callaway-Sant'Anna chapter summarizes ideas that take Brantly Callaway and Pedro Sant'Anna 30 pages of formal proof in Journal of Econometrics to establish; the AMM chapter compresses Hayden Adams's Uniswap v3 whitepaper into half an hour of reading. If you intend to actually defend the paper, you must also read those primary sources — this textbook tells you which ones, in what order, and exactly what to extract from each.

It is also not neutral. Throughout, we flag the places where this specific project is on weak ground — small N, endogenous listing timing, BUIDL's near-zero-velocity pathology — and we tell you what to say when a reviewer raises them. The honest answer to "is this paper publishable in JFE?" is "no, and that's fine, because Finance Research Letters and Ledger are the right targets."

Chapter 01 · Part I — The Problem

The puzzle of idle tokens

A $25-billion asset class where most of the assets sit, week after week, doing absolutely nothing.

The headline number, and what it hides

In May 2026, the total market capitalization of tokenized real-world assets crossed $25 billion. The largest single instrument, BlackRock's USD Institutional Digital Liquidity Fund — ticker BUIDL — alone holds roughly $2.4 billion in tokenized U.S. Treasuries. That number is real: every BUIDL token is backed by an actual short-duration Treasury bill held in BNY Mellon custody, and the Securitize transfer agent will redeem it on demand for an institutional client. This is not a stablecoin pegged to nothing. This is a securitized claim on government debt, issued on Ethereum, by the world's largest asset manager.

Now look at how often it trades.

As of the most recent week of data, BUIDL has approximately 85 holders. Not 85 million, not 85 thousand. Eighty-five wallets. In a typical week, the number of secondary on-chain transfers between non-issuer addresses can be counted on one hand. The annualized turnover ratio — the fraction of outstanding supply that actually changes hands in a year — is below 1%. For comparison, a typical U.S. equity ETF turns over its float more than ten times per year. The 10-year Treasury itself turns over roughly fifteen times.

So the central empirical puzzle of this project is:

The puzzle

Tokenization was sold to investors and regulators as a path to dramatically more liquid secondary markets for traditionally illiquid assets. Putting a Treasury on Ethereum is supposed to mean it trades 24/7, atomically, with low friction. In practice, most of these tokens trade less than the underlying instruments do. Why? And does it change when the issuer puts the token on a Uniswap pool?

What "velocity" actually means here

Throughout this textbook the word velocity means one very specific thing: the annualized dollar value of secondary-market transfers, divided by the token's average market capitalization over the same period.

Written out:

$$\text{velocity}_{i,t} = \frac{\text{secondary transfer volume}_{i,t} \times 52}{\text{average market cap}_{i,t}}$$

The factor of 52 annualizes a weekly observation. "Secondary" means we exclude mints (creation of new tokens by the issuer), burns (destruction at redemption), and transfers to or from a hand-curated list of issuer, custodian, and protocol-internal wallets. Chapter 5 is entirely devoted to building this measure rigorously. For now, the point is:

  • Velocity of 0 means the token sat in its existing wallets all week.
  • Velocity of 1 means the equivalent of the entire outstanding supply turned over once during the year.
  • Velocity of 10 means it turned over ten times — close to a liquid ETF.

Most of our 7 tokens, most weeks, sit between 0 and 0.5. A few, occasionally, spike to 5 or 10 around a specific event. USDY, the most retail-accessible name, occasionally reaches 20 in promotion weeks. The dispersion across tokens and across weeks is huge — and that dispersion is what will give us identifying variation when we get to the regression.

The intervention: AMM listing

Of the seven tokens, only three received their first listing on a major decentralized exchange's automated market maker (AMM) within our sample window of January 2023 through April 2026. They are:

  • OUSG — Ondo Short-Term U.S. Government Bond, first listed on Uniswap v3 in late 2023.
  • USDY — Ondo's yield-bearing dollar product, listed on Uniswap v3 in spring 2024.
  • STBT — Matrixdock's Short-Term Treasury Bill Token, listed in a Curve stableswap pool in 2024.

The other four — BUIDL, BENJI, USTB, USYC — were never AMM-listed in our sample. They serve as our never-treated control group. The question is straightforward:

The causal question

For the same token, what does its weekly secondary-market velocity look like in the weeks after AMM listing, compared to a plausible counterfactual where listing did not happen? And does the size of the AMM pool's available liquidity (its TVL, total value locked) modulate the size of the effect?

Why this is harder than it sounds

A first-year graduate student in economics would look at the question above and reach for a simple "before vs. after" comparison: take the average velocity of OUSG in the 8 weeks before its Uniswap listing, subtract from the average in the 8 weeks after, and report the difference. This is exactly the wrong approach, for at least four reasons that this textbook will unpack carefully:

  1. Selection on the trend. Ondo did not choose to list OUSG on Uniswap at a random moment. They listed it precisely because demand was building. Some of the post-listing velocity increase reflects momentum that was already there.
  2. Common shocks. What if the entire DeFi market — and with it, all RWA tokens — got more active during the same weeks for unrelated reasons? A FOMC rate cut, a major BlackRock announcement, a Coinbase listing event. We need to net out time effects that hit every token equally.
  3. Persistent differences. BUIDL is structurally a low-velocity token because of its institutional-only KYC restrictions. USDY is structurally higher because it's retail-facing. Comparing the two without netting out token-level fixed effects will smuggle in those structural differences and call them "treatment effects."
  4. Staggered treatment. OUSG was treated first, then USDY, then STBT. It turns out that the standard "two-way fixed effects" panel regression — the workhorse of empirical economics for forty years — can give a single coefficient that is a confused weighted average of treatment effects, in which some of the implicit comparisons use already-treated units as controls and subtract their dynamic effects in the wrong direction. The repair for this is one of the most important methodological developments of the last decade, and it is the methodological heart of the project. We spend three chapters (8, 9, 10) on it.
