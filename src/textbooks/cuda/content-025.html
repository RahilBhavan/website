6.4The CUTLASS Connection

The library that does what you just did, in templates

Every pattern in this textbook — tiled blocking, register accumulators, vectorized loads, mma.sync, ldmatrix, cp.async pipelining — is implemented in NVIDIA's open-source CUTLASS library. CUTLASS is an aggressively templated C++ header collection that lets you compose GEMMs and convolutions out of reusable tile- and warp-level pieces.

If your goal is shipping a fast GEMM in production rather than learning, CUTLASS is your starting point. If your goal is learning, you have done the hard part already, and reading CUTLASS source becomes legible — every WarpMma, ThreadblockMma, and PipelinedMma template you encounter is a name for a pattern this book just walked you through.

What to look at first

  1. cutlass/include/cutlass/gemm/device/gemm.h — the high-level entry point, parametrized by tile shapes, dtypes, and pipeline stages. Reading the template parameters is half the education.
  2. cutlass/include/cutlass/gemm/warp/mma_tensor_op.h — the warp-level mma.sync wrapper. Compare against your Listing 3.4-B.
  3. cutlass/include/cutlass/arch/mma_sm80.h — per-architecture mma.sync intrinsics. Read this and the matching PTX side-by-side.
  4. The cute sub-library (CUTLASS 3.x). A layout algebra that lets you describe register/SMEM/global mappings as composable tensors. The mental model is more powerful than anything in CUTLASS 2.x and is the right next investment if you intend to write production kernels.
  5. The CUTLASS profiler — runs every internal kernel against your shape and reports TFLOPS, giving you instant comparable numbers across hundreds of tile configurations.

Repository: github.com/NVIDIA/cutlass. The accompanying NVIDIA Developer Blog posts give a guided tour.[3]

6.5What Comes Next

Five projects, each one a credible follow-up
ProjectWhy it's a good next stepKey references
Flash Attention Apply the same IO-aware tiling philosophy to attention. The S = Q·KT matrix is never materialized; softmax, masking, and Q·K → P·V fuse into a single kernel. The result was a ~3× wall-clock speedup over PyTorch attention and is now standard in every modern transformer stack. Dao et al., FlashAttention (NeurIPS 2022); FlashAttention-2 (2023); FlashAttention-3 (2024).
Quantized GEMM (INT8, FP8, INT4) Modern LLM inference runs in INT4/INT8; the cuBLAS/cuDNN paths are less mature than FP16. Plenty of room to win, and the techniques (per-channel scaling, group quantization, lookup-table mul) generalize. SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ papers; llama.cpp CUDA backend; bitsandbytes.
Stream-K GEMM An alternative work decomposition: tile along K instead of M/N, so all SMs share the K-reduction and stay busy on awkward shapes where M/N tiling leaves SMs idle. CUTLASS implements it. Worth reading and reimplementing. Osama et al., "Stream-K" (PPoPP 2023); CUTLASS Stream-K examples.
Persistent kernels Instead of launching a fresh kernel per work item, launch one kernel that loops over work items pulled from a queue. Amortizes launch cost; enables cross-kernel state in registers/SMEM. Triton and CUTLASS persistent-kernel docs.
Multi-GPU GEMM (NCCL, NVLink) Sharding a GEMM across 8 GPUs requires reasoning about communication overlap, ring/all-reduce primitives, and NVLink bandwidth. The tile-level mental model from this book transfers; the systems-level concerns are new. NCCL documentation; Megatron-LM tensor parallelism; PyTorch FSDP source.
Triton kernels OpenAI Triton lets you write GPU kernels in a Python DSL that compiles to PTX. The performance ceiling is close to hand-written CUTLASS for many shapes. Once you know what good PTX looks like, reading Triton-generated code becomes diagnostic rather than mysterious. Triton tutorials at triton-lang.org; the flash-attn Triton reference.

None of these are a step backward in difficulty from the cuBLAS project. Pick the one whose application area you care about (attention if you care about LLMs, quantized GEMM if you care about inference deployment, multi-GPU if you care about training infrastructure) and execute it with the same protocol discipline as chapter 5. The point of this textbook is not just to ship one fast kernel — it is to give you the toolset to ship the next ten.

A useful career-shaped observation
The community of people who can write production GPU kernels is small enough that one credible, reproducible, well-documented kernel project changes how interviewers, collaborators, and senior engineers treat you. The leverage is real. Ship the repo.
