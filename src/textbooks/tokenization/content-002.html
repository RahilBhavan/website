Value capture and valuation discipline

After this chapter: Trace fees to the specific holder claim before using a valuation model.

Watch for this mistake: Gross protocol fees are token-holder earnings.

A useful product need not imply a valuable token. Draw the route from customer payment to the token holder. Gross fees first pay someone: validators, lenders, liquidity providers, employees or a treasury. A protocol retaining revenue does not necessarily distribute it to holders. A vote about future distributions is weaker than an enforceable present entitlement.

Separate value creation from capture. Value creation is benefit to customers and counterparties. Capture is what the particular instrument receives under its rules. A token may be necessary for collateral without granting profits. Demand can also be speculative; label it instead of presenting it as recurring operating income.

Worked example: users pay $10 million a year. Suppliers receive $7 million. Operations cost $2 million. The remaining $1 million enters a treasury. A $100 million token market cap divided by $10 million gross fees produces 10x, but that is not a holder earnings multiple. Even 100x the residual is meaningful only after establishing rights, sustainability and dilution. A discount model of hypothetical holder distributions is an assumption-sensitive scenario, not a universal fair value.

Burning removes units under contract rules. It can benefit remaining units under some demand assumptions; it does not create customer revenue by itself. If 8 million units are issued and 3 million burned over a year, net outstanding issuance is 5 million. A price rise is not guaranteed: demand and expectations can move in the opposite direction.

Fresh check T3: users pay $12 million; providers receive $9 million; operations cost $2 million. Governance holders currently have no distribution right. What is the residual, who owns it, and which additional evidence would justify treating any of it as holder cash flow? Do not silently capitalize all fees.

Close the chapter. Try a new case.

Fees are $15M, supplier costs $10M and operating costs $3M. Assume enforceable terms distribute half the residual to this token class. Find the class distribution before other costs or dilution.

Reveal this case’s solution

Residual is $2M. Under the stated hypothetical entitlement, the class receives $1M. A real product still requires verification of that entitlement and its conditions.

Check the core idea

Which evidence would make fee revenue relevant to this specific token's cash flows?

An actual routing or distribution mechanism plus the relevant legal rights, operating costs, dilution and authority to change the mechanism.

Rewards, security and reflexivity

After this chapter: Separate token rewards, ownership change and dollar return.

Watch for this mistake: A 10% token reward means a 10% investment return.

Ask who funds every yield. Sources can include borrowers, transaction users, asset income, treasury subsidy, new issuance and new deposits. Reward tokens are not costless merely because no bank transfer occurs. Their issuance changes ownership and their dollar value changes with price.

Worked example: you hold 100 of 1,000 outstanding tokens and receive 10 over a year. Your balance rises 10%. If total supply becomes 1,200, your share becomes 110/1,200 = 9.17%, down from 10%. If the token falls from $2 to $1, ending value is $110 versus $200 initially, before any fees or taxes. Token yield, ownership share and dollar return answer different questions.

Proof-of-stake systems use capital at risk and protocol penalties to support correct behavior. Specific issuance, inactivity penalties, slashing and exit rules differ by chain and version. Ethereum's own documentation is the appropriate starting point for its mechanism [5]. Staking through a service adds provider and operational risks; a liquid receipt can trade away from its redeemable value.

Reflexivity is a feedback loop in which market value changes behavior or backing, which changes market value again. Suppose a token supports collateral value and pays operators. A falling price can lower collateral coverage and operator compensation simultaneously. More issuance may cover a dollar budget at the cost of greater dilution. Analyze the loop before relying on a static coverage ratio.

Fresh check T4: hold 200 of 2,000 units; receive 20; ending supply 2,400; price falls from $5 to $4. Calculate balance growth, ownership share and dollar return. Name one reason a posted yield may not persist.

Close the chapter. Try a new case.

You start with 80 tokens at $5, receive 8 more, and finish at $3 per token. Supply rises from 800 to 1,000. Find dollar return and ending ownership.

Reveal this case’s solution

Ending value is $264 versus $400 initially: -34%. Ownership is 88/1,000 = 8.8%, down from 10%.

Check the core idea

Name the three different quantities a reward changes or interacts with.

Your token balance, your fraction of total supply, and the dollar value of that balance. Token price and total issuance can move independently.

Liquidity, AMMs and market structure

After this chapter: Calculate a pool trade and distinguish its average price from its ending quote.

Watch for this mistake: The initial marginal quote applies to the entire order.

A market cap uses a marginal price. Liquidity asks how much can actually trade and at what cost. A quote, a bid-ask spread, available depth and the expected execution cost of your trade are distinct. Daily volume can include repeated trades and does not prove useful depth.

In a simplified constant-product automated market maker, reserves x and y satisfy x*y=k. The reserve ratio indicates a marginal price before fees. An order moves that ratio. This is a teaching model of a particular AMM structure, not every exchange; Uniswap v2's whitepaper is the reference [6].

Worked example without fees: a pool contains 1,000 X and 100,000 dollars, so k = 100 million. Add $10,000. New dollar reserve is $110,000; new X reserve is 909.09. The buyer receives about 90.91 X and pays an average $110/X. The initial marginal price was $100; the final marginal price is about $121. The average execution price and final quote are different.

Liquidity providers bear inventory exposure. Arbitrage can bring pool prices toward outside markets, but it transfers value from mispriced reserves and does not guarantee the providers a profit. For a fee-free 50/50 constant-product pool compared with holding its initial assets, relative impermanent loss at price ratio r is 2*sqrt(r)/(1+r)-1. At r=4 it is -20%; trading fees and other implementation details alter actual results.

MEV concerns value extracted through transaction inclusion, ordering or exclusion. A quoted swap can execute badly if market state changes. Slippage limits bound acceptable execution but can cause failure; they do not eliminate all manipulation. Liquidity mining can rent deposits that leave when incentives end.

Fresh check T5: a fee-free pool has 500 X and $50,000. Add $5,000. Compute X received, average execution price and final marginal price. Explain why a $10 million market cap cannot establish that a $1 million sale will execute near the quote.

Check the core idea

Why does buying from a constant-product pool change the next unit's price?

The trade changes reserve quantities. The marginal reserve ratio moves as the buyer removes one asset and adds the other.
