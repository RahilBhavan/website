19. Learn to reject misleading shortcuts

  • “BTC Yield is 10%, so I earned 10%.” It describes a company ratio, not your total return.
  • “They bought more Bitcoin, so each share is worth more.” Check share issuance, senior claims, costs, and the purchase price.
  • “No margin call means no risk.” Repayment, liquidity, custody, and market-price risks remain.
  • “Perpetual means principal is safe.” It can mean there is no scheduled ordinary repayment.
  • “Cumulative means cash is guaranteed on schedule.” Accumulation and payment are different rights.
  • “Bitcoin-backed means I have a lien on Bitcoin.” Economic exposure is not the same as a legally secured claim. Read the instrument.
  • “BTC Rating is a credit-agency rating.” It is the company’s own framework, not an external agency rating. Dashboard definitions
  • “High GAAP profit means plenty of cash for dividends.” Check the cash-flow statement and unrealized asset-value movements separately. The issuer itself identifies unrealized Bitcoin gains/losses and accounting changes as relevant risks. Offering disclosures

20. Tax and accounting claims require another layer

A return-of-capital distribution and a taxable dividend can have different consequences. “Tax-equivalent yield” depends on assumptions about the investor and distribution treatment. It is not the cash paid, and “tax deferred” does not mean “tax free.” Inspect the issuer’s Form 8937 material and the rules relevant to your own account and jurisdiction rather than importing a promotional yield comparison. Issuer’s return-of-capital archive

For company analysis, read four statements together: income statement, balance sheet, cash flows, and the debt/preferred footnotes. Identify unrealized changes, actual sales, financing proceeds, and cash available for use. A complete current valuation would also need software economics, tax effects, and every material claim. This guide teaches how to ask those questions; it does not supply a full audited valuation model.

22. Read the two books in dialogue

Start with the authorized Broken Money sample. In the inspected banking chapters, separate an underlying asset from a claim issued against it. Ask: whose asset is someone else’s liability? A fast message confirming payment is not necessarily final settlement of the underlying asset. Alden uses the gap between communication and settlement speeds to explain the growth of intermediaries. That is an interpretive framework, not proof that one technology determines all monetary outcomes.

Then read the authorized Bitcoin Standard sample. Focus on indirect exchange, acceptance across people and places, and the stock of an asset relative to new production. The stock-to-flow idea describes supply behavior; it does not independently determine a market price.

Compare their questions: Alden asks how monetary infrastructure and claims evolve; Ammous emphasizes which goods resist supply expansion. Both help explain Bitcoin advocacy. Neither sample establishes Strategy’s cost of capital or MSTR’s fair value.

Optional full-book assignments, identified from their tables of contents rather than claimed as reviewed:

  • Broken Money: banking chapters 7–8, money creation chapter 15, debt cycles chapter 19, and crypto risks chapter 26.
  • The Bitcoin Standard: government money chapter 4, time preference chapter 5, digital money chapter 8, and objections chapter 10.

For every chapter, record one useful definition, one author claim, one supporting observation, and one objection. Page numbers can differ by edition; use chapter names and your own copy’s contents.

28. Your ongoing research worksheet

For any article, podcast, dashboard, or earnings call, fill in:

  • Observation date / publication date: both matter.
  • Claim: copy the idea in your own words.
  • Type: definition, observation, forecast, metaphor, or opinion.
  • Evidence: exact filing, contract section, dataset, or timestamp.
  • Assumptions: financing access, conversion, price, tax, timing, and costs.
  • Affected holder: common, a particular preferred, a creditor, or direct Bitcoin owner.
  • Disconfirming observation: what would make you change your view?
  • Unresolved: what could not be accessed or checked?

Stop collecting resources once you can trace the mechanism and identify the remaining unknowns. More interviews do not substitute for checking the actual claim.

29. Retrieval practice

Close the guide and answer these questions before reading the key. This is practice, not a claim of mastery or a financial suitability assessment.

  1. Name the three separate propositions behind Bitcoin, Strategy, and an individual security.
  2. Why does a fixed supply fail to guarantee a rising Bitcoin price?
  3. What is the difference between a payment message and settlement of the underlying asset?
  4. Who owns the Bitcoin if you own MSTR? Can you redeem the stock for coins?
  5. A company has 100 BTC and 100 shares. It issues 10 shares at $200 while Bitcoin is $100. What happens to BTC per share?
  6. Repeat question 5 with a $50 share issue price.
  7. Why might investors buy a zero-coupon convertible? What does the issuer give up?
  8. How can a preferred issuance increase gross BTC per common share without increasing simplified net value?
  9. What is the difference between cumulative and non-cumulative dividends?
  10. A $100-stated-amount preferred pays a 10% annual rate. You pay $80. What is the simple current yield? Is it your guaranteed return?
  11. Net value per share rises from $100 to $120. The valuation multiple falls from 2× to 1×. What is the price return?
  12. Bitcoin assets are $100, senior claims $30. Bitcoin falls 50%. What happens to the simplified residual?
  13. Why can five years of dividend/interest reserve coverage still coexist with a near-term liquidity problem?
  14. Which share count should you use for gross BPS versus a net measure that deducts out-of-the-money claims?
  15. What must you check before saying a proposed daily dividend is already effective?
