Holder counts vs. velocity — the deep correlation

One pattern to flag now, because it will matter for identification: holder count and velocity are positively but imperfectly correlated. USDY has 12,000 holders and high velocity; BUIDL has 85 holders and near-zero velocity. But the relationship is not mechanical — USYC has only 180 holders yet sees significant weekly volume because those holders are mostly DeFi protocols rebalancing inventory. This means we have to include $\log(\text{holders})$ as a time-varying control in the regression, otherwise the AMM-listing effect could be confounded by holder-base growth that AMM listings themselves accelerate (a mechanism — interesting in its own right, examined in Chapter 15).

With the institutional landscape in hand, the next chapter turns to the infrastructure that the treatment variable is built around: how AMMs actually work, especially for assets that trade within a tight range around $1.

Chapter 03 · Part II — Infrastructure

AMMs for fixed-NAV assets

From x·y=k to concentrated liquidity to stableswap — the pricing functions that determine what "AMM listing" actually means in our regression.

Why we have to understand the price function

The treatment variable in this project is "the token has been listed on a major AMM with at least some available depth." That phrase hides three decisions: what counts as a "major AMM", how to measure "depth", and what threshold of depth counts as a meaningful listing. To make these decisions rigorously, you need a working understanding of how AMM pricing functions differ. Two pools with the same reported "TVL" can offer wildly different effective liquidity for an RWA token that trades within a few basis points of $1. The pricing function determines that.

We'll cover three pricing families in this chapter:

  1. Constant product ($x \cdot y = k$) — the original Uniswap v1/v2 design.
  2. Concentrated liquidity (Uniswap v3) — same invariant locally, but each LP picks a price range.
  3. Stableswap (Curve) — a different invariant entirely, optimized for assets that should trade near par.

Of our three treated tokens, OUSG and USDY sit in Uniswap v3 pools (concentrated liquidity); STBT sits in a Curve stableswap pool. That difference is what makes STBT an internal robustness check for the project.

What an automated market maker actually is

A traditional order-book exchange matches a buyer's bid against a seller's ask. Liquidity is whatever sits in the limit order book at any moment. There is no liquidity if no one has posted an order.

An AMM removes the order book. Instead, two reserves of two tokens sit in a smart contract, and a fixed mathematical relationship between those reserves determines the price of one token in terms of the other. Anyone can deposit equal value of both tokens to provide liquidity; anyone can swap one token for the other against the pool, paying a small fee that accrues to the liquidity providers (LPs). There is always a price, because the formula always defines one.

For a constant-product AMM holding reserves $x$ of token A and $y$ of token B, the pool maintains the invariant:

$$x \cdot y = k$$

$k$ stays approximately constant across swaps (it actually grows slightly as fees accrue). If a trader wants to buy $\Delta y$ of token B, they must deposit enough of token A to keep $k$ unchanged. The required deposit is:

$$\Delta x = \frac{k}{y - \Delta y} - x$$

The marginal price (the price of an infinitesimal trade) is simply $p = y / x$. The realized price (the price you actually pay for a finite trade) is worse than the marginal price — and the gap is called slippage. Slippage is large when the trade is large relative to the pool's reserves, and small when the pool is deep.

Why constant product is wrong for RWAs

Constant product is the right design for volatile pairs (ETH/USDC, where the price can move 50% in a week). It spreads liquidity uniformly across all possible prices from $0$ to $\infty$, so no matter where the price ends up, there's some liquidity there.

For a tokenized Treasury, this is wasteful. OUSG trades within a few cents of its NAV, week after week. An LP supplying a constant-product pool deposits capital that will sit at price points like $\$0.50$ or $\$5$ — prices that will never be reached. That capital earns no fees because no swaps happen there. To get a meaningful amount of liquidity available at prices near $\$1.00$, an LP would have to deposit enormous amounts of capital.

Uniswap v3 fixed this with concentrated liquidity: each LP picks a price range $[p_l, p_h]$, and their capital is only active within that range. Within the range, the LP behaves exactly like a constant-product LP for a hypothetical pool with "virtual" reserves; outside the range, the LP is entirely in one asset.

The key consequence for our project: a Uniswap v3 OUSG/USDC pool with $100k of TVL concentrated in the range $[\$0.99, \$1.01]$ provides roughly 50× the effective depth of a v2-style pool with the same nominal TVL — and a tighter range of $[\$0.998, \$1.002]$ pushes that to roughly 250×. This is why a "small" v3 pool by reported TVL can still meaningfully facilitate trading.

The stableswap invariant (Curve)

Curve's stableswap, introduced by Michael Egorov in 2020, uses a different invariant altogether — one designed from the start for assets that should trade near a fixed relative price. The invariant is:

$$A n^n \sum_i x_i + D = A D n^n + \frac{D^{n+1}}{n^n \prod_i x_i}$$

Don't memorize that. What matters is the shape: stableswap is a hybrid between a flat constant-sum curve (which would be perfect for assets at exactly equal price but offers no protection if they diverge) and a constant-product curve. The amplification coefficient $A$ controls how flat the curve is near the balanced point. High $A$ means very flat (very low slippage near equal balance) but sharp curvature once balance is disrupted; low $A$ means the curve behaves more like constant product everywhere.

In a USDC / STBT Curve pool with $A=100$, a $\$50k$ swap near balance might have slippage of one or two basis points. In a hypothetical Uniswap v2 pool with the same $\$2M$ TVL, the same swap would suffer something like 50 basis points of slippage. Curve's design is roughly $100×$ more capital-efficient near the peg, which is exactly what an RWA token needs.

What "AMM TVL" actually means in our regression

Now that you understand the three pricing functions, two methodological points from the blueprint should make sense:

  1. The treatment threshold of $\$10k$ TVL is venue-aware. A $\$10k$ v3 pool tightly concentrated around $\$1.00$ provides meaningful depth for a few-thousand-dollar trade. A $\$10k$ v2 pool would provide essentially nothing. We deliberately set the threshold low so that early v3 listings count as "treated" even when their nominal TVL is modest; in Chapter 14 we run a robustness check at $\$50k$ and $\$100k$.
  2. STBT's Curve TVL is double-sided and must be halved. DefiLlama reports the total dollar value of both sides of a stableswap pool (STBT + USDC). For comparability with the Uniswap pools, we use only the RWA-side TVL — implementation detail flagged in data_collection/03_pull_amm_pools.py.

The economic intuition for why AMM listing matters

With this background, the project's core hypothesis becomes economically clear. Before AMM listing, the only way to acquire OUSG is through Ondo's primary issuance channel: complete the KYC process, sign the subscription agreement, wire funds, and receive tokens 1-3 business days later at the day's NAV. This is a primary-market transaction, and the friction (time, paperwork, minimum sizes) limits who participates.

After AMM listing, any wallet on the allowlist can swap USDC for OUSG in a single atomic transaction with no minimum size, paying a few basis points of fees plus slippage. The friction collapses. We would expect this to:

  • Increase the rate of secondary trades (the velocity we measure).
  • Increase the rate at which the holder base churns (people enter and exit).
  • Increase the volume of "rebalancing" trades — small swaps in and out as DeFi protocols and individual holders adjust positions.

Those three mechanisms together are the economic content of "AMM listing causes velocity to rise." The regression measures the magnitude. Chapter 15 revisits whether we can separately identify the three mechanisms (we cannot with this data; we can suggest paths forward).

Chapter 04 · Part II — Infrastructure

On-chain data foundations

Where transfer events come from, what's actually in a Transfer log, and the decision tree that separates a "real" trade from a custodial reshuffle.

What "on-chain data" actually is

Every transaction submitted to an Ethereum-compatible chain emits zero or more logs. Logs are the chain's way of publishing structured side-effects of a transaction — they are not stored in account state, but they are stored permanently in the chain's block history and are cheap to filter and retrieve. The ERC-20 standard requires that any token transfer emits a specific log called a Transfer event, with three fields: from address, to address, and value.

Every secondary-market trade we want to count is a Transfer log. Every mint is also a Transfer log (with from = 0x000…000). Every burn is a Transfer log (with to = 0x000…000). Every internal custodial movement at Securitize is also a Transfer log. This is good — we get a complete record of every movement. It is also bad — we have to filter aggressively to separate the kind of movements we want from the kind we do not.
