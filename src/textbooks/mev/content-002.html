Measuring loss: the counterfactual

Why you need this“Loss” needs a definition someone can check. You need to say what is compared with what.

(a) Counterfactual loss, the headline. Take the pool state at the start of the block. Replay the block exactly, with every attacker swap and attacker liquidity change removed. The victim's loss is what it would have received in that replay minus what it actually received.

(b) Attacker gross. Every WETH and USDT transfer between the pool and the attacker's two transactions: $8,713 for the week, before $4,400 of gas.

(c) Tolerance available. The no-attack output minus the victim's own minimum output, read from the router call. Defined for 115 victims: $1,582 of tolerance, of which attackers took $1,544 (98%).

Values are in USD at the price at the start of the block, with USDT taken as $1. The median victim lost $14.14; the 20 largest sandwiches carry 64% of the week's loss.

Say it in one breathLoss is the difference between the victim's real output and an exact replay of the same block without the attacker.

Check yourself

  1. Why is (b) not equal to (a)?

    The attacker's gain and the victim's loss are measured against different baselines, and some value goes to fees and liquidity providers. Here (b) was $8,713 and (a) was $12,387.

  2. Why remove the attacker's liquidity changes too?

    A world without the attack has no JIT liquidity either. Keeping it would cushion the victim and understate the loss.

  3. What does 98% of tolerance tell you?

    Attackers size the front-run to the victim's limit. The limit, not the trade size, sets how much a victim can lose.

Chapter 7

Mechanism-design vocabulary

Why you need thisKe Wu's paper is written in this language. You need these four terms to read it and discuss it.

  • Order: (type, amount v, limit rate r). For example, buy up to v ETH, paying at most r USDC per ETH.
  • Arbitrage resilience: no group of orders can end with a gain in one token and no loss in the other. That is, no risk-free profit, even for a block producer who inserts, drops or reorders orders (Definition 2, Theorem 3.1).
  • Incentive compatibility (IC): reporting your true order is your best strategy; no lie, split, delay or fake order makes you better off (Definition 3). IC implies arbitrage resilience.
  • Plain model and weak fair-sequencing model: what a strategic player can do to the order of transactions.

The paper compares outcomes with a partial ordering, not a single utility number: an outcome is better if you get more of what you wanted at a price within your limit. Some outcomes are not comparable, for example getting more ETH than you asked for.

Say it in one breathArbitrage resilience means nobody can make risk-free profit from the batch; incentive compatibility is the stronger claim that honesty is your best strategy, and it needs fair sequencing.

Check yourself

  1. Is a sandwich an arbitrage in the paper's sense?

    Yes. The attacker ends with no net ETH and more USDC, a risk-free gain, which arbitrage resilience forbids.

  2. Why does IC need the fair-sequencing model?

    In the plain model, ties are broken by arrival time, and a block producer can fake an early arrival to get a better place. Fair sequencing removes that move.

  3. Which property did the study test numerically?

    Arbitrage resilience: in every run the attacker's two legs net to zero or less.

Chapter 8

The Chan–Wu–Shi two-phase mechanism

Why you need thisThis is the design the email is about. You need to run it by hand on a small block.

The mechanism clears all of one block's orders for one pool together (Figure 1 of the paper):

  1. Fix r0, the pool's marginal price at the start of the block. Drop any order whose limit is worse than r0.
  2. Measure each order's size in ETH at r0. The side with more volume is dominant; the other is the minority.
  3. Phase 1: fill the whole minority side at r0 against the dominant side, in tie-break order. The pool does not move.
  4. Phase 2: the rest of the dominant side trades on the curve one order at a time, each until it is filled or the marginal price reaches its limit.

Why the sandwich fails. In phase 1 everything trades at r0. In phase 2 only the dominant side trades, and each order pays r0 or worse. So the attacker's buy pays at least r0 and its sell gets at most r0. It cannot end with a risk-free gain. That is Theorem 3.1, and the study's code checks it in every run.

Why the real result is 52–99%, not 100%

The mechanism removes the profit. It does not stop a trade placed ahead of the victim from moving the price in phase 2. The study gave each attacker leg its own executed price as a limit, a price the attacker is known to accept. Then:

  • The back-run's limit is worse than r0, so step 1 drops it.
  • The front-run trades on the curve until the marginal price reaches its own average price, about half its size.
  • The victim, behind it, still pays for that half.

In the toy this recovers 50% of the victim's loss; in the real week, 52%. Random tie-breaking puts the victim first about half the time, giving 75% on average. If the attack stops because it no longer pays, the victim keeps about 100%.

Average limits against marginal limits

Uniswap users state an average limit (a minimum output). The paper's limit is marginal. Reading the toy victim's minimum (8.8 ETH for 20,000 USDC) as a marginal limit fills only 83% of its order, because its own trade pushes the marginal price past 2,273 USDC per ETH. That is the question the email to Ke Wu asks.

Say it in one breathMatch buys against sells at the start-of-block price, then run the leftover one-sided flow along the curve; the attacker's two legs can only trade at r0 or worse, so the sandwich has no profit left.
