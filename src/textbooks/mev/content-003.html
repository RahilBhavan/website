Check yourself

  1. In phase 1, does the pool's price change?

    No. Buyers and sellers trade with each other at r0, and the pool's reserves are untouched.

  2. Why can't the back-run sell above r0?

    The sell side is the minority, so it trades only in phase 1, at exactly r0. Only the dominant side trades along the curve.

  3. Why does the study report a range, not one prevented share?

    The result depends on how the attacker's orders would look under the mechanism (limits, ordering) and whether the attacker attacks at all. The range shows that dependence.

  4. Is this a uniform-price batch auction?

    No. Phase 2 orders pay different prices. The paper says uniform pricing alone does not give incentive compatibility, and the Trilemma paper proves you cannot have both with weak local efficiency.

Chapter 9

The Trilemma

Why you need thisThe follow-up paper (Li, Shi & Zhang, 2026) tells you what the mechanism had to give up. It is the natural next question in a meeting.

  • IC: incentive compatibility (chapter 7).
  • wLE, weak local efficiency: no "reasonable" order (limit at least as good as r0) is left unfilled when the pool's ending price would still have suited it.
  • UP, uniform pricing: every executed order trades at the same price.

A uniform-price batch auction (CoW-style) gets wLE and UP, but the paper shows a user with real demand can gain by misreporting. So it is not IC. Uniform pricing removes the pure sandwich, but not every strategic gain.

The trilemma paper also notes the cost of the drop rule in step 1: an order to buy below r0 is ignored even if the pool's ending price would have suited it. In the study, the smallest victim fill in the arrival-order run was 83%.

Say it in one breathYou can have any two of honesty-is-optimal, no wasted reasonable demand, and one price for everyone, but not all three; Ke Wu's mechanism keeps the first two.

Check yourself

  1. Which property does Chan–Wu–Shi give up?

    Uniform pricing.

  2. Why isn't a uniform-price batch auction enough?

    It stops zero-demand arbitrage like sandwiches, but a trader with real demand can still gain by misreporting, so it is not incentive compatible.

  3. What is the main open question both papers leave?

    Achieving IC with weak local efficiency in the plain model, without assuming fair sequencing.

Chapter 10

Wellman's lens: batch against continuous

Why you need thisWellman has no blockchain papers. The connection is his work on how market rules change strategic behavior.

In a continuous market each order trades on arrival, so being first is worth money. Wah & Wellman (2016) simulate fragmented stock markets and find that frequent call markets, which collect orders and clear them together at intervals, eliminate latency arbitrage. A block is a natural batch interval.

Empirical game-theoretic analysis (EGTA) is Wellman's main method. Simulate a market with many strategies, estimate the payoff of each combination, and find the equilibrium among them. The study's biggest limit is exactly an EGTA question: it replays the same orders and cannot say how bots, builders and traders would change their behavior under the new rule.

Say it in one breathContinuous trading pays whoever is first; batching removes that prize, and how traders re-optimize under the new rule is an empirical game.

Check yourself

  1. What does a frequent call market remove, according to Wah & Wellman?

    Latency arbitrage: profit from reacting to information or orders faster than others.

  2. Why is the 52–100% range an EGTA question?

    The range depends on what attackers do once the attack stops paying. Working that out means finding the equilibrium of the new game, which is what EGTA does.
