Why this matters: what reviewers care about

Two reviewers, three concerns. Standard pushback on a DiD paper with N=7:

  1. "How do you know the effect isn't just noise?" Answer: the placebo test. Random treatment assignment produces a distribution centered near zero with the same level of within-cluster noise as our actual data. If the actual ATT sits in the right tail of that distribution, the noise explanation is implausible.
  2. "How do you know the effect isn't driven by one outlier token?" Answer: the leave-one-out check. Re-run the main spec dropping each token in turn. If the result is stable across all 7 drops, no single token is doing the work. Reported in the appendix; brief discussion in the robustness section.
  3. "How do you know listing isn't endogenous to pre-trends you can't measure?" Answer: HonestDiD bounds. Even allowing the post-treatment PT violation to be as large as the largest observed pre-treatment violation, the CI still excludes zero. This is the strongest version of the parallel-trends defense available with this sample.

A note on what NOT to call robustness

A common trap in empirical work: running the same specification with slightly different controls and calling each one a "robustness check". This is not robustness — it's specification fishing, and reviewers spot it immediately. A real robustness check perturbs one structural choice (the DV, the treatment, the sample, the estimator) and keeps everything else constant. Then you can interpret any differences as informative about that one choice.

Our eleven robustness specs each perturb a single dimension. They are not "specifications I happen to like best"; they are pre-specified probes of the most likely concerns a reviewer will raise.

Limitations and improvements

The four major limitations that bound what this paper can claim, and the concrete improvements that would let a follow-up paper claim more.

Limitation 1: N=7 makes statistical power low

We have said this many times in this textbook, but it deserves one more direct treatment. With 7 clusters, 170 weeks, and the noise levels we observe in pilot data, our expected power to detect a 30% velocity-increase ATT at the 5% level is around 45%. For a 50% increase, power is around 65%. For a 100% increase, power is around 85%.

What "45% power" means in practice: if the true ATT is 30% and we ran this exact study 100 times with fresh data each time, we would detect a significant effect in about 45 of those 100 studies. The other 55 would report a confidence interval that includes zero — a "null result" — even though the true effect is real and 30% in magnitude. With one study, we have a coin-flip chance of detecting an effect of plausible magnitude. That is the honest constraint.

Figure 15.1 — Power calculator

Power estimate uses a normal-approximation formula with a design-effect adjustment for within-cluster correlation: power ≈ Φ(|ATT| / SE − 1.96) where SE = σ·√((1 + (T·prop − 1)·ρ) / (N·T·prop)) and ρ ≈ 0.3 is the assumed intraclass correlation; prop is the share of post-treatment observations. Slider defaults (3 treated, 4 controls, T=170, ATT=0.40, σ=0.60) reproduce the chapter's headline ~45% figure.

Three concrete improvements that buy us power:

  • Add 8–10 more tokens. The natural expansion targets are TBILL (OpenEden), WTGXX (WisdomTree), TBILL+ (Backed), USTBL / EUTBL (Spiko), USDM (Mountain), bIB01 (Backed Treasury Bond), and HKTBILL (Hong Kong-issued T-bill token). Doubling N from 7 to ~15 roughly doubles power at fixed effect size.
  • Switch to daily panels. Multiplying T from 170 to 1,190 adds a $\sqrt{7}$ factor to power. Daily aggregation introduces more noise per cell but the increased T usually wins for moderate effects.
  • Expand to multi-chain. OUSG on Mantle, BUIDL on Arbitrum/Avalanche, USDY on Aptos and Solana, BENJI on Stellar. Each chain-deployment of the same underlying token is partially a separate observation, especially for the treatment-vs-no-treatment comparison (some chains have AMM listings, others don't, of the same token).

Limitation 2: AMM listing timing is endogenous

We have flagged this throughout. Ondo did not pick a random week to list OUSG on Uniswap — they picked the week when they were ready, when their compliance sign-offs were in, and (most concerning) when their growth trajectory suggested the pool would attract enough volume to justify the LP incentive program. Some of the post-listing velocity rise reflects pre-listing momentum that would have continued anyway.

We address this within the data via three mechanisms — parallel pre-trends, the placebo test, and HonestDiD bounds — but none of them are a true instrumental variable. The fundamental answer is "we cannot fully break the endogeneity with this data; what we can say is that the post-listing pattern is consistent with a treatment effect of the magnitude reported, and is inconsistent with several alternative explanations."

A follow-up paper could pursue genuine instruments. Two plausible candidates:

  • The Uniswap Foundation's RWA pool incentive program. In late 2024 Uniswap allocated grants to specific RWA pool LPs. The timing of grant approvals (which involve Foundation governance votes that have idiosyncratic delays) is plausibly exogenous to the underlying token's trajectory. If we can collect grant timing data, that becomes a candidate first-stage instrument.
  • Regulatory clarifications. The SEC's various 2024 and 2025 statements on tokenized money market funds shifted the calculus for AMM listing. Some statements affected some issuers but not others. A cross-issuer comparison around the regulatory event might give us a quasi-IV.

Neither instrument is clean enough for a top-five journal, but either would push the paper from "consistent with a treatment effect" to "instrumental variables identify a treatment effect of X". That is the difference between a working paper and a JF submission.

Limitation 3: Multi-chain scoping (BENJI in particular)

We scoped BENJI to its Polygon deployment, ignoring Stellar where most of its supply sits. This was a pragmatic choice driven by Stellar's lack of a mature getAssetTransfers-equivalent API. The cost: BENJI is a partial unit in our panel, providing a never-treated control but with much smaller transfer volume than its true global activity.

An obvious extension is to build a Stellar Horizon pipeline. This is roughly 4–6 weeks of engineering effort. The Horizon API does return operations list-by-account, so the basic data is available — what's missing is the Alchemy-style bulk filtering that makes the EVM pipeline efficient. A dedicated Stellar effort would essentially involve writing a custom transfer collector that walks the operations log block by block, an order of magnitude slower than the EVM pipeline. We don't do it now because the payoff is one extra never-treated unit (Stellar BENJI is still never AMM-listed). It would be more worthwhile if a multi-chain follow-up paper aimed to study a network of cross-chain bridged tokens specifically.

Limitation 4: Zero-inflated dependent variable

Forty percent of token-week observations have $V_{i,t} = 0$. Our main spec handles this with the IHS transform, which is defined at zero. The two-part hurdle model is reported as robustness. But neither approach gives us a clean decomposition of the AMM effect into:

  • Extensive margin: AMM listing makes any trade more likely in a given week (turns zero weeks into positive weeks).
  • Intensive margin: Conditional on a trade happening, AMM listing makes the trade bigger.

Distinguishing these is interesting in its own right because they suggest different economic mechanisms. The extensive margin is consistent with AMM listing reaching new traders who otherwise would not have transacted at all; the intensive margin is consistent with existing traders simply trading more efficiently. With a panel large enough to support the two-part model as the main spec, we could separately report both. Currently we cannot.

Two improvements that don't require a new paper

Some of the limitations can be partially addressed by additional analyses within the same paper. Two worth doing before submission:

  1. Leave-one-out sensitivity. Run the main CS estimator 7 times, each time dropping one token. Report the seven estimates. If the result is stable across all 7 leave-outs, no single token is driving the headline. Implementation is one R loop; reported in appendix.
  2. Cohort heterogeneity table. The CS estimator naturally produces three cohort-specific ATTs (one for OUSG, USDY, STBT). Reporting these separately answers the natural follow-up question — "does the effect differ by issuer / venue type?" Likely finding: USDY's effect is largest (most retail-facing), STBT's smallest (Curve, smaller pool, institutional).

How to position limitations in the paper

The biggest mistake authors make in the limitations section is being defensive. The reviewer's job is to find limitations; if you've named them yourself, you've reframed the conversation. Our limitations section will:

  • State each of the four major limitations in the same plain language used in this chapter.
  • Quantify each one where possible (e.g., "power for a 30% ATT is approximately 45%").
  • Describe the specific follow-up paper or extension that would address it.
  • Not apologize. Limitations of one paper are research opportunities for the next.
