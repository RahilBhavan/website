8.1 Precision, and why the base rate decides it

Precision is the share of alerts that are real: true alerts ÷ all alerts. It's the headline because every false alert costs an investigator time. Recall is the share of real kit sites that alert. The trap is the base rate. If only 1 in 100 fetched pages is a kit site, even a rare false positive happens to the 99 others, so false alerts can outnumber real ones.

8.2 Six labels, and why decoys don't count as wins

Each alert gets one label in ssrd review: scam-kit, not-scam, decoy-cloaked, parked, error or unsure. Precision is scam-kit ÷ all labeled alerts. A decoy might well be a scam, but you didn't see the scam, so it counts as not confirmed, and the decoy share is reported beside precision. Label from the sanitized text, the matched signals and the nearest kit member. Write down your rules for each label before you start, so the second labeler gets the same instructions.

8.3 Intervals: why 13 of 19 isn't simply "68%"

A proportion from a small sample is uncertain. The textbook interval p ± 1.96·√(p(1 − p)/n) (the Wald interval) misbehaves when n is small or p is near 0 or 1. It can run past 100%, and its real coverage falls well short of 95%. The Wilson interval fixes this by solving for the range of true proportions that would make your result unsurprising. It's what evaluate.wilson computes:

centre = (p + z²/2n) / (1 + z²/n) and half = z·√(p(1−p)/n + z²/4n²) / (1 + z²/n), with z = 1.96.

8.4 Kappa: do two people agree beyond luck?

If you label your own alerts, a skeptic can say the precision is circular. The SPEC answer: a second labeler independently labels 50 of the same items, and you report Cohen's kappa, κ = (po − pe) ÷ (1 − pe). po is how often you agree. pe is how often you'd agree by chance, given how often each of you uses each label.

8.5 Never let the tuning data grade you

The thresholds marked "placeholder" (TLSH 70, Jaccard 0.80, copy 30) get chosen in week 3 by sweeping values on labeled data. Whatever data you sweep on, you'll fit, including its noise. So the precision you report must come from different alerts, labeled after the threshold was frozen. CLAUDE.md puts it as a rule: never tune thresholds on the evaluation set.

8.6 Confirmation you didn't produce: the CBR forward check

evaluate.cbr_forward counts SSRD candidates that CBR lists on or after the day SSRD first saw them, and how many of those SSRD alerted on. It's independent of your labels, so it answers the circularity objection from a different direction. It confirms "a regulator later listed this domain", not "this matches kit X", and CBR's listing delay is unknown, so a short run will undercount.
