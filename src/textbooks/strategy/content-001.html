Fact 3: it is very volatile

Bitcoin has fallen by more than 70% from a peak several times, and has more than doubled in a year several times. For a company, that means the value of its main asset can halve while its dollar obligations stay the same. Volatility also has an upside: it makes options on MSTR valuable, which is why convertible buyers accepted 0% interest (Chapter 1, idea 7).

Why a company, not just an ETF?

Since January 2024, anyone can buy bitcoin through US spot ETFs for a small fee. So why would an investor hold MSTR instead? Strategy's answer has three parts:

  1. Leverage without margin calls. MSTR holders get more than 1x bitcoin exposure through Strategy's debt and preferreds, and nobody can force them to sell in a crash.
  2. Growth in bitcoin per share. An ETF share always holds the same amount of bitcoin. A Strategy share can hold more over time if the company sells stock at a premium (Chapter 6).
  3. Products ETFs can't offer. Income securities (the preferreds) and volatility (MSTR options, converts) carved out of the same bitcoin.

The counter-argument is just as simple. The ETF has no debt, no dividends to fund, no management discretion, and trades at its asset value. If Strategy's premium disappears, the main reason to prefer it weakens (Chapter 20).

Custody and control

Strategy holds its bitcoin through institutional custodians. Its convertible notes are unsecured, and its filings describe the preferreds as not collateralized by the bitcoin. That's a choice with history. In 2021–22 it did borrow against bitcoin, and the 2022 crash made that debt a liability (Chapter 5). Unpledged coins can't be seized in a margin call. They also give preferred holders no direct claim on the bitcoin. Their cushion is the company's overall balance sheet.

Check yourself

Why can't Strategy pay dividends "out of" its bitcoin without selling it?

Bitcoin produces no income. Dividends are dollars (or euros), so they must come from cash on hand, new securities sold, the small software business, or selling bitcoin.

Give one reason to own MSTR rather than a bitcoin ETF, and one reason not to.

For: leveraged bitcoin exposure that can't be margin-called, plus potential growth in bitcoin per share. Against: you pay a premium, take on dividend and debt obligations, and depend on management's decisions.

Chapter 3

Strategy in one page

Strategy is a company that turns money raised in the capital markets into bitcoin, then sells new kinds of securities whose value rests on that bitcoin.

It used to be MicroStrategy, an enterprise software company. It still runs that software business, but the software is now a rounding error next to the balance sheet. In October 2026 Strategy held 848,250 BTC, worth about $69.7B at $82,131. That is more than any other company.

To understand it, think of three jobs it does at once:

  1. Bitcoin treasury. It owns the bitcoin and wants more of it for every share of common stock.
  2. Issuer of "digital credit." It sells five preferred stocks (STRF, STRC, STRE, STRK, STRD) and convertible notes. Those pay fixed or variable income, and the bitcoin is the cushion behind them.
  3. Treasurer. It has to keep enough US dollars on hand to pay about $1.56B a year in dividends and interest, because bitcoin pays nothing.
The one ideaStrategy is a bet that bitcoin will rise faster than the cost of the money used to buy it. Common stock holders own the upside. Preferred and note holders get paid first, in dollars, from a company whose main asset pays no dollars. Nearly every question about Strategy is a version of "is that still working?"

Check yourself

Why does Strategy need a USD Reserve at all?

Bitcoin produces no cash flow and the software business is small, so dividends and interest have to come from cash raised earlier, new securities sold now, or selling bitcoin. The Reserve is the first of those, kept separate so investors can see it.

Who owns the upside if bitcoin doubles?

Mostly MSTR common holders. Preferred holders get their fixed dividends either way (STRK also has a conversion option into MSTR).

Chapter 4

How it got here

The story has three phases. From 2020 to 2024 Strategy bought bitcoin with common stock and cheap convertible debt. In 2025 it launched a preferred stock almost every few months. In 2026 the focus shifted to defending those preferreds: a cash reserve, a higher STRC rate, buybacks and new rules for when it will sell stock.

The patternEach new product answered a problem the last one created. Converts needed MSTR to keep rising; preferreds reached investors who wanted income; STRC's variable rate let Strategy adjust the price of money monthly; the Reserve answered "where do the dividends come from?"; buybacks and issuance discipline near 1x answered "what if the stock trades below its bitcoin?"

Chapter 5

Every big decision, and why

Strategy didn't arrive at five preferreds and a cash reserve by design. Each step solved a problem the previous step created. This chapter walks through fourteen decisions in order: the problem, the options, the reason given, and what it cost.
