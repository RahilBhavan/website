Uniswap v3: concentrated liquidity

Why you need thisThe study replays every swap with Uniswap v3's exact integer math. You need to say what that math tracks and why exact replay is the gate.

In Uniswap v3, liquidity providers put money in a chosen price range. Inside a range the pool behaves like a constant-product pool with that much liquidity. Ranges are built from ticks: each tick is a 0.01% price step. When a swap moves the price across a range boundary, the active liquidity changes and the price can move faster or slower.

The contract stores the price as sqrtPriceX96: the square root of the price, times 296, as an integer. Every Swap event records the price, active liquidity and tick after the swap. That is why the study can rebuild the pool state from logs alone.

The replay gate. The study ported Uniswap's integer math to Python and replayed all 70,669 swaps of the week. Every one matched exactly: the amounts and the price, liquidity and tick afterwards. Three broken inputs make it fail, which shows the check can catch errors: a wrong fee breaks 70,645 swaps, dropping 20 ticks breaks 1,284, and ignoring the 129 liquidity changes breaks 334.

Just-in-time (JIT) liquidity. A bot can add liquidity in a narrow range right before a big swap and remove it right after, collecting the fee. In 37 of the 236 sandwiches the attacker did this inside the attack. Chapter 4 shows how.

Say it in one breathUniswap v3 is a stack of constant-product pools over price ranges; the study reproduces every swap of the week to the last unit before it trusts any what-if.

Check yourself

  1. Why replay every real swap before computing any counterfactual?

    If the simulator matches reality exactly on 70,669 swaps, a difference in the counterfactual comes from the change you made (removing the attacker), not from a simulator error.

  2. What does a tick crossing change?

    The active liquidity. Liquidity providers whose range starts or ends at that tick join or leave.

  3. Why do negative controls matter?

    A gate that never fails proves nothing. Breaking the fee, the tick data or the liquidity events makes it fail, so it really checks the inputs.

Chapter 4

The sandwich attack

Why you need thisThis is the problem the paper solves. You need the mechanics, and the reason the minimum output is the attacker's budget.

The victim sends "buy ETH with 20,000 USDC, at least 8.8 ETH". An attacker sees it and puts three transactions in order:

  1. Front-run: buy ETH first, pushing the price up.
  2. Victim: buys at the higher price and gets less ETH.
  3. Back-run: sell the ETH from step 1 at the even higher price.

The minimum output is the budget. The attacker makes the front-run as large as possible while the victim's swap still succeeds. In the toy, a larger front-run would push the victim below 8.8 ETH and the victim's transaction would revert, ending the attack. So the attacker uses all of the victim's tolerance. The real data shows the same: attackers took 98% of the tolerance victims allowed. Victims with no minimum at all (29 decoded cases) can lose almost everything. One wallet put 0.447 WETH into the pool and got 3.89 USDT back.

The JIT variant. jaredfromsubway's front-run sometimes also adds liquidity in the single tick where the victim will trade, and the back-run removes it. The victim then trades at the pushed price against the attacker's own liquidity.

Say it in one breathThe attacker buys in front of you, lets you buy at the higher price, then sells back, and it sizes the front-run to push you exactly to your minimum.

Check yourself

  1. Why doesn't the attacker use an even bigger front-run?

    The victim's transaction would fall below its minimum output and revert. The back-run would then sell into a market the victim never pushed up.

  2. Why does setting a tight slippage limit protect you?

    It shrinks the attacker's budget. A 0.1% tolerance leaves almost nothing to take after fees and gas.

  3. What is the attacker's profit made of?

    The price difference between the front-run's buy and the back-run's sell, which the victim's trade created.

Chapter 5

Finding sandwiches in the data

Why you need thisA professor's first question will be how you know these are sandwiches. You need the rule, its tiers and how it was checked.

The detector reads each block's swaps on the pool in log order. It merges each transaction into one leg and looks for this pattern:

  • a front-run and a back-run in the same block, front-run first, in opposite directions;
  • the same attacker: the same sending address, or the same bot contract with amounts within ±1%;
  • the back-run sells 90–110% of what the front-run bought (the rule from Qin, Zhou & Gervais, S&P 2022);
  • at least one victim between them, trading in the front-run's direction, from a different sender and contract.

How it was checked. The evidence for 30 random hits was read row by row: each leg's full receipt, how many senders use each contract, and ENS names resolved on chain. 25 were textbook sandwiches, 3 were losing attempts, and 2 had front-runs that also traded in other pools. Then 15 Etherscan pages were read for 5 hits. All 5 are sandwiches, and the attacker contracts carry Etherscan's "MEV Bot" tags. The check also found the JIT pattern.

Dune's rule, re-implemented. Dune's public spellbook rule (same sender, no amount check) was run locally on the same logs. It confirms 235 of the 236 sandwich blocks and flags 20 more, all with back-run amounts outside the 90–110% window. So the headline count is conservative. No Dune account was used.

What it misses: sandwiches that span two blocks or several pools, and attacks on private flow whose cause cannot be seen.

Say it in one breathA sandwich is the same attacker trading both ways in one block around a victim, with the back-run undoing the front-run, and the result holds in its strictest tier.

Check yourself

  1. Why require the back-run to sell 90–110% of what the front-run bought?

    It separates an attacker closing its position from two unrelated trades by a bot that trades both ways all day.

  2. Why report three tiers?

    So the headline doesn't depend on one threshold. The strictest tier still has 178 sandwiches and $10,043 of loss.

  3. Name one kind of attack the detector cannot see.

    A sandwich whose legs are in different blocks, or one that front-runs through another pool.
