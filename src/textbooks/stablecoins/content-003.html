Why this cannot be a causal claim

  • Two episodes. Two data points cannot separate a rate effect from anything else that happened.
  • Anticipation. Markets price cuts before they happen, so the 12-week baseline may already contain the response.
  • Confounders. An election (November 2024), a law (July 2025) and shifting appetite for crypto all moved in the same windows.
  • Autocorrelation. Weekly flows are not independent, so "every other window" is a loose yardstick.
  • Choosing the windows after seeing the cuts. The comparison starts from the event, which flatters any pattern.
  • Short histories. USDY starts December 2025 and has no episode comparison. BUIDL starts March 2024.
  • Measurement error. Block-to-time interpolation has up to an hour of error. The newest week is dropped as partial.

The honest phrase is "descriptive." Use it.

In this repo: netSeries(), episodeResults(), PRE_WEEKS, TAIL_WEEKS in src/lib/issuance.ts. The method is written out in docs/METHODOLOGY.md under "Event comparison".

6

Interest rates and why they might move flows

Leave with

  • The Fed sets a target range for its policy rate. The study uses the upper bound and the dates the changes took effect.
  • Lower rates make a zero-yield stablecoin cheaper to hold, and a Treasury fund less attractive. They also move risk appetite.
  • These channels push in different directions. The data can show direction, not cause.

The vocabulary

The federal funds rate is the interest rate at which banks lend reserves to each other overnight. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) sets a target range for it, such as 4.25% to 4.50%. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percentage point, so a "25 bp cut" lowers the range by 0.25 points. Treasury bills are short-term US government debt; their yield tracks the expected path of the policy rate closely.

The study uses the FRED series DFEDTARU, the upper bound of the range. FRED dates a change by the day it took effect, which is the day after the FOMC announcement.

The two easing episodes

The study joins cuts that came less than 120 days apart into one easing episode. That gives two:

  • Episode 1: Sep 2024 to Dec 2024. 3 cuts, 100 bp in total, first effective Sep 19, 2024.
  • Episode 2: Sep 2025 to Dec 2025. 3 cuts, 75 bp in total, first effective Sep 18, 2025.

The series also shows a 25 bp increase effective 17 September 2026. It is not analyzed. If someone asks, say it is recorded and outside the study.

Why a cut could change flows

Channel 1: the opportunity cost of holding a stablecoin

A stablecoin pays holders 0%. Cash elsewhere pays the Treasury bill rate. Holding the stablecoin therefore costs the rate each year. When rates fall, that cost falls. Standard money-demand logic says people hold more of a good when its holding cost drops, so net issuance of payment stablecoins may rise.

Channel 2: the yield advantage of the fund

A Treasury fund pays about the bill rate. Against a zero-yield stablecoin its advantage is the rate itself. When the rate falls, the advantage narrows, and fund demand may fall relative to stablecoins.

Channel 3: risk appetite

Cuts can lift stock and crypto prices. If traders then want more crypto exposure, they mint stablecoins to fund purchases. This works the same way as channel 1 on stablecoins but it is a different story and cannot be told apart in these data.

Channel 4: the issuer

Lower rates cut issuer income (chapter 2). That could change how aggressively issuers court new customers, but the logs cannot see it.

Anticipation

Markets move before the Fed does. Yields fall as soon as traders expect cuts, so the 12 weeks before the first cut may already contain the effect. The baseline is therefore not "before the policy". Whenever you describe the study, say it compares flows in windows around the dates, not the effect of the decision.

What about rate increases?

The sample also contains tightening. There were four hikes between February and July 2023, and one hike effective 2026-09-17. The same method applies, with two limits. Our data begin in January 2023, so the 2023 window has no 12-week baseline, and it overlaps the Silicon Valley Bank run (chapter 7), which dominates USDC that spring. The September 2026 window is only a few weeks old: the data end before the four-week tail, so the figure marks it partial.

Read this as a record of what happened, not a test. If cuts raised stablecoin issuance and lowered Treasury-fund issuance, a symmetric story would say the reverse in tightening. The 2023 window cannot show it, because of the bank run. The 2026 window is too short to show anything. Say that when asked about hikes.
