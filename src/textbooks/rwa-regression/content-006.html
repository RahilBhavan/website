Three building-block 2×2 comparisons

With three cohorts (early-treated E, late-treated L, never-treated N), TWFE computes three types of 2×2 DiD inside itself:

  1. Treated vs. never-treated. Compare E (or L) to N over a consistent window. These are clean: N never changes status, so the parallel-trends assumption can hold.
  2. Early vs. late, BEFORE late is treated. Use E as treated, L as control, over the window where L is still "not yet treated". This is also clean.
  3. Late vs. early, AFTER early is treated. Use L as "treated" (correct), E as "control" — but E has been treated for a while and its outcome is now reflecting a treatment effect. Using E as a control here is what Goodman-Bacon calls the forbidden comparison.

Type 3 is the killer. The TWFE machine cannot distinguish "early-treated unit's changing outcome" from "control unit's expected counterfactual change". It treats E's post-treatment dynamics as if they were E's untreated trajectory. Then when L's treatment kicks in and L's outcome rises faster than E's (which is plateauing), the comparison subtracts E's continued rise — pulling the estimate of L's effect downward, sometimes flipping its sign.

The result, in one sentence

TWFE = (weighted avg of clean 2×2 comparisons) − (weighted avg of forbidden 2×2 comparisons that subtract dynamic treatment effects). Under dynamic treatment effects, TWFE is biased toward zero or even toward the wrong sign.

The implications for our project

Our three treated cohorts (OUSG, USDY, STBT) are exactly the staggered structure that creates forbidden comparisons. And we have strong prior reasons to expect dynamic treatment effects — AMM TVL builds gradually over weeks after a pool is created, and the velocity response should plausibly grow as more LPs provide liquidity and more traders find the pool.

If we ran TWFE blindly on this panel:

  • The OUSG effect would partially be estimated using post-listing USDY and STBT as "controls" (in the forbidden direction), pulling the estimate toward zero.
  • The USDY effect would be estimated using already-treated OUSG as a "control", biased in the same direction.
  • The single TWFE coefficient $\hat\beta$ would be a confused mixture, not interpretable as any single token's treatment effect.

We still report TWFE in the paper, as Table 2 — but as a comparison baseline, not the preferred specification. The blueprint's analysis/02_twfe_baseline.R runs the regression and reports the coefficient alongside the result of bacondecomp::bacon(), which decomposes the TWFE estimate into its building blocks and reports the weights. If the forbidden comparisons (using already-treated units as controls) account for a non-trivial fraction of the total Bacon weight (we expect 15–25% in this sample), that is itself a finding worth showing a reviewer — it justifies the move to Callaway-Sant'Anna.

The exact Bacon weighting formula

Goodman-Bacon proves that the TWFE coefficient under staggered treatment equals:

$$\hat\beta_{\text{TWFE}} = \sum_{k} w_k \cdot \widehat{\text{ATT}}_k^{2\times 2}$$

where the index $k$ runs over all possible 2×2 comparisons embedded in the panel and $w_k$ are non-negative weights that sum to 1 (in the sense that the comparison receives a positive weight in the decomposition). The bias arises because the "treatment" assignment in some of those 2×2 comparisons is wrong: an already-treated unit being used as control means the comparison's $\widehat{\text{ATT}}^{2\times 2}$ equals (true ATT of late cohort) MINUS (dynamic-effect change of early cohort over the same window). Subtracting a positive dynamic-effect contamination pulls the reported "ATT" downward.

The fix is to never use treated units as controls. Callaway-Sant'Anna does exactly this by construction: it builds $\widehat{\text{ATT}}(g, t)$ as a direct DiD between cohort $g$ at period $t$ and the never-treated (or not-yet-treated) units only, with no aggregation that could re-introduce contamination. That's the topic of Chapter 9.

What you should remember

  • TWFE under staggered treatment ≠ a simple average of treatment effects.
  • The bias comes from using already-treated units as implicit "controls" for later-treated ones.
  • Bias is worst when treatment effects are dynamic (grow over time after treatment).
  • We use TWFE as a comparison only and run the formal Bacon decomposition to expose the bias mechanically.
  • The fix is Callaway-Sant'Anna, which never lets a treated unit serve as control.
Chapter 09 · Part III — Causal Inference

The Callaway-Sant'Anna estimator

A treatment-effect estimator that — by construction — never uses a treated unit as a control. The methodological backbone of the project.

The atomic building block: ATT(g, t)

Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) sidestep the TWFE problem by defining a more granular estimand. Rather than collapse the entire panel into one coefficient, they define the average treatment effect for each (cohort, calendar period) cell:

$$\text{ATT}(g, t) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_t(g) - Y_t(\infty) \mid G_i = g\right]$$ where $Y_t(g)$ is the potential outcome at time $t$ for a unit first treated at time $g$, and $Y_t(\infty)$ is the never-treated potential outcome.

Here $g$ is the period in which a unit was first treated (its cohort), and $t$ is calendar time. $\text{ATT}(44, 60)$ would read as "the average treatment effect on units that were first treated in week 44, evaluated in week 60". For our project, $g \in \{44, 68, 92\}$ (the three treated cohorts) and $t$ runs from 1 to 170. So the full set of $\text{ATT}(g, t)$ values forms a grid with about 400 cells worth estimating (each cohort $g$ contributes estimates for every $t \geq g$).

How each ATT(g, t) is estimated

For a chosen cohort $g$ and period $t \geq g$, the CS estimator constructs the simplest possible 2×2 DiD:

$$\widehat{\text{ATT}}(g, t) = \left[ \bar Y_{g, t} - \bar Y_{g, g-1} \right] - \left[ \bar Y_{\mathcal C, t} - \bar Y_{\mathcal C, g-1} \right]$$

where the bars denote sample means, the first bracketed term is the change in cohort $g$'s outcome between its last pre-treatment period $g-1$ and the current period $t$, and the second bracketed term is the analogous change in a chosen control group $\mathcal C$. There are two natural choices for $\mathcal C$:

  • Never-treated (control_group = "nevertreated"): $\mathcal C$ = the units that are never treated within the sample window. In our project, that's BUIDL, BENJI, USTB, USYC.
  • Not-yet-treated (control_group = "notyettreated"): $\mathcal C$ = units that have not been treated by period $t$. So when estimating $\text{ATT}(44, 50)$, the eligible not-yet-treated control units include USDY (will be treated at 68, still untreated at 50) and STBT (will be treated at 92, still untreated at 50). When the estimator later evaluates $\text{ATT}(44, 70)$, USDY is excluded from the control group because it became treated at 68.

The two choices have a tradeoff. Never-treated is the most conservative — those units' parallel-trends assumption is the cleanest because they are never disturbed by treatment. But never-treated has fewer units, and they may differ structurally from the treated units in ways that violate parallel trends (BUIDL is institutional-only; OUSG is not). Not-yet-treated has more units and better matches the treated cohort structurally — they are the kind of tokens that did end up getting AMM-listed — but the eligible control pool shrinks as $t$ grows, and the ATT(g, t) for very long horizons may rest on a thin control set.

Our main spec uses never-treated for conservatism. We report not-yet-treated as a robustness check in Chapter 14.

Aggregation: from many ATTs to one number

A grid of 400 cells is not what we report in a paper. The CS framework provides principled aggregation rules that collapse the grid into one of several summary objects. The did::aggte() function in R offers four:

  • type = "simple" — a weighted average of all post-treatment ATT(g, t), weighted by cohort size. Returns one number, the overall ATT.
  • type = "dynamic" — re-indexes time as $e = t - g$ (event time, weeks since treatment), and reports ATT at each event time. This is the most useful aggregation for a paper because it produces an event-study plot.
  • type = "group" — reports a separate ATT per cohort. Useful for asking "is the OUSG effect different from the USDY effect?"
  • type = "calendar" — reports ATT per calendar period, averaged across cohorts. Useful for asking whether the treatment effect varies across macro regimes (low-rate vs. high-rate weeks).

Our paper reports all four. The headline result in Table 3 is from type = "simple"; the event-study plot in Figure 2 is from type = "dynamic"; the cohort heterogeneity discussion uses type = "group".

The event-study plot, interactively

The event-study aggregation is the single most informative plot in the project. The x-axis is event time (event-time 0 is the listing week; -8 is 8 weeks before; +12 is 12 weeks after). The y-axis is the estimated ATT, with confidence bands. The plot tells you three things at once: (1) the pre-treatment ATTs should hover around zero — that's the pre-trends test visualized; (2) the post-treatment ATTs show how the effect builds up over time; (3) the confidence bands tell you how precisely each ATT is estimated.
