Estimation methods: regression vs. inverse-probability weighting

The CS framework allows two estimation methods for each ATT(g, t), controlled by the est_method argument in att_gt():

  • "reg" (regression adjustment) — fits a linear regression of $Y_t - Y_{g-1}$ on the controls in the control group, then uses that fitted model to impute the counterfactual change for the treated cohort. Sensitive to model misspecification but works well in small samples.
  • "ipw" (inverse probability weighting) — fits a propensity-score model for being in cohort $g$, then weights the control group by inverse propensity scores. More robust to outcome model misspecification but requires the propensity-score model to be correctly specified.
  • "dr" (doubly robust) — combines both, providing consistent estimates if either the outcome model or the propensity-score model is correctly specified.

Our main spec uses "reg" for simplicity and small-sample stability. The doubly robust estimator is reported as a robustness check, but with only 7 units the propensity-score component is too underpowered to add much. Chapter 14 reports the comparison.

Three things that can go wrong

  1. Insufficient never-treated controls. If for some period $t$ the never-treated group is empty (e.g., all controls had left the sample), $\widehat{\text{ATT}}(g, t)$ cannot be computed for that $t$. With 4 never-treated controls in our sample, this is not a concern, but it would be in a panel where all units eventually get treated.
  2. Empty cohort cells. If a cohort $g$ has only one unit (as is the case for each of our cohorts — OUSG alone is cohort 44), the within-cohort variance cannot be estimated and CS will return point estimates with very wide confidence intervals. Wild cluster bootstrap (Chapter 11) is the right inference procedure in this case.
  3. Covariate model misspecification. The xformla argument controls which covariates enter the conditional parallel-trends regression. If we omit a relevant covariate that varies across cohorts in a way that correlates with treatment timing, the resulting ATT(g, t) estimates are biased. Our spec includes the obvious time-varying controls but does not include token-specific interactions; reviewer-anticipated robustness check.

Chapter 10 · Part III — Causal Inference

Sun-Abraham and friends

The other modern staggered-DiD estimators, what each does differently, and why we use Sun-Abraham specifically as a robustness check.

The family of modern estimators

Goodman-Bacon's 2021 paper made the TWFE problem visible. Within roughly two years, four research teams independently proposed estimators that solved it. Their frameworks differ in details, but each one shares the same core insight: never use already-treated units as controls. The four are:

  • Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) — ATT(g,t) building blocks, aggregated. Our main estimator.
  • Sun & Abraham (2021) — Interaction-weighted estimator, implemented as a TWFE regression with cohort-by-relative-time interactions.
  • Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024) — "Imputation estimator": fit untreated units only, predict counterfactual for treated, take the difference.
  • de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfœuille (2020, 2022) — Cohort-specific DiD with explicit handling of treatment effect heterogeneity; implemented in the R/Stata package did_multiplegt.

How they relate

Under "standard" conditions (no anticipation, parallel trends, no controls), the four estimators are approximately equivalent — they target the same population estimand and differ only in their efficiency and small-sample behavior. They can diverge under one of three conditions:

  1. Different conditioning sets. CS conditions on covariates via either regression adjustment or IPW; SA conditions parametrically via the TWFE regression specification. If the chosen covariate model differs, the estimators differ.
  2. Different control groups. CS lets you pick never-treated or not-yet-treated explicitly. SA's fixest::sunab() implementation uses the never-treated cohort as the omitted reference when one exists, and otherwise the last-treated cohort. Borusyak-Jaravel-Spiess use all not-yet-treated observations.
  3. Different aggregation weights. SA's interaction-weighted aggregation puts more weight on cohorts with more pre/post observations. CS's type = "simple" aggregation uses uniform weighting. The two can differ by 5–15% in samples like ours.

Sun-Abraham, specifically

Sun-Abraham (SA) is the most pedagogically useful alternative because it lives inside the TWFE framework. You write down a TWFE regression but replace the single treatment dummy with a saturated set of cohort-by-event-time interactions:

$$Y_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \delta_t + \sum_{g} \sum_{e \neq -1} \beta_{g,e} \cdot \mathbb{1}[G_i = g] \cdot \mathbb{1}[t - g = e] + \varepsilon_{i,t}$$

The coefficients $\beta_{g, e}$ are direct estimates of the cohort-specific event-time effects. To produce an overall event-time path, SA averages $\beta_{g, e}$ across cohorts at each event time $e$, weighted by cohort size. Because the "treatment" indicator is now cohort-specific, the forbidden-comparison problem vanishes — there is no longer a single $\beta$ that pools across cohorts.

In fixest (R) the SA estimator is one line:

)

      main = "Sun-Abraham event study",
      xlab = "Weeks relative to first AMM listing",
      ylab = "ATT on IHS(velocity)")

Why we report both

CS and SA, fit to the same panel with the same covariates and the same control group, should produce numerically similar point estimates. If they do, that is strong evidence that the result is not driven by a peculiarity of one estimator's implementation. If they disagree substantially, that's a flag — usually pointing to an issue with the not-yet-treated vs. never-treated distinction or to a sample where the propensity-score model is unstable.

Our project's Table 4 reports CS as the headline, with SA in column 2 for direct comparison. The expectation is point estimates within ±10% of each other and overlapping confidence intervals. If they don't agree, that becomes a finding worth discussing in the text rather than burying.

Brief look at the other two estimators

Borusyak-Jaravel-Spiess "imputation estimator"

The cleanest conceptual estimator. Fit a model of $Y_{i,t}$ on token FEs, time FEs, and covariates, using only the untreated observations. Use the fitted model to predict the counterfactual outcome $\hat Y_{i,t}(0)$ for treated observations. The estimated treatment effect for unit $i$ at time $t$ is then $Y_{i,t}^{\text{obs}} - \hat Y_{i,t}(0)$.

Implemented as the did_imputation R package or as didimputation in Stata. Conceptually attractive but in our sample tends to produce wider confidence intervals than CS because of how it propagates uncertainty from the imputation step. We do not report it in the main paper, but it is in analysis/05_robustness.R for completeness.

de Chaisemartin-D'Haultfœuille

Focuses on cohort-specific DiD with explicit allowance for treatment-effect heterogeneity in arbitrary directions. Implemented as did_multiplegt. Most useful when treatment is continuous (a dose, an intensity), which is the case for our $\log(\text{AMM TVL})$ specification but not the binary $\text{amm\_listed}$ spec. We report it as a sensitivity check specifically for the continuous-treatment regression in Chapter 13.

Reviewer-anticipation: which alternative will they ask for?

In a journal review, the most likely methodological pushback will be: "Why CS and not Sun-Abraham?" The defensible answer is the one above — they should agree, and reporting both is sufficient. A more aggressive reviewer might ask about Borusyak-Jaravel-Spiess specifically, because it has the cleanest theoretical foundation; the answer there is that we ran it, the point estimate is similar but with a wider confidence interval, and we discuss the cost-benefit in an appendix.

With the menu of estimators established and CS chosen, the next chapter turns to the inference problem that lurks under all of them: with only 7 clusters, the conventional cluster-robust standard errors are unreliable. The fix is the wild cluster bootstrap.

Chapter 11 · Part III — Causal Inference

Inference with seven clusters

Why conventional cluster-robust standard errors lie when N is small, and how the wild cluster bootstrap (with Webb weights) tells the truth.

Why we cluster at the token level in the first place

Two velocity observations from OUSG — say, week 50 and week 51 — are not independent. They share the same issuer, the same investor base, the same KYC regime, the same recent news. The residuals from any regression we fit are therefore correlated within token. Failing to account for this correlation leads to standard errors that are too small — the regression "thinks" each token-week is an independent draw, when really there are far fewer "independent observations" than the raw count suggests.

The standard correction is cluster-robust standard errors (CR-SEs), introduced by Liang and Zeger (1986) and routinely used in panel data since Bertrand, Duflo and Mullainathan (2004). They reweight the variance computation to allow arbitrary correlation within each cluster. In our regression, the clusters are tokens — so we report standard errors clustered at the token level. That's the meaning of cluster = ~token_id in the fixest call and of vcov = "cluster" in the did call.

All good — except CR-SEs have a known asymptotic property: they require the number of clusters to be "large", in some informal sense. The standard rule of thumb is N ≥ 30 clusters. Our N is 7.

What goes wrong at N=7

Cameron and Miller (2015) is the canonical practitioner's guide to this problem. They identify three things that go wrong with conventional CR-SEs when N is small:

  1. Downward bias. The estimator $\hat V_{\text{CR}}$ tends to underestimate the true variance, by a factor that depends on N and on the balance of the panel. With N=7 the bias can easily be 20–40%.
  2. Wrong-shape sampling distribution. The standard t-test assumes the test statistic follows a $t$ distribution with N-1 degrees of freedom. With N=7, this is $t_6$ — a distribution with very fat tails. p-values computed from a standard normal (or even from $t_6$) reject the null too often.
  3. Sensitivity to outlier clusters. If one of the 7 clusters has an unusually large or small residual, the CR-SE jumps. With more clusters, outlier effects average out. With 7, they don't.

The practical consequence: a study with N=7 that reports a 95% confidence interval using conventional CR-SEs is probably reporting something more like an 80% interval in reality. Type I error rates can be 15–25% rather than the nominal 5%.
