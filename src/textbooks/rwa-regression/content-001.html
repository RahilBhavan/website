Chapter 02 · Part I — The Problem

The seven-token universe

Who issues each, who can hold each, what trades and what doesn't, and why these seven and not others.

What is a tokenized Treasury, exactly

A tokenized U.S. Treasury is an ERC-20 (or, in BENJI's case, an asset on Stellar or Polygon) whose underlying backing is a portfolio of short-duration U.S. Treasury bills held in segregated custody at a regulated bank or trust company. Each unit of the token represents a fractional ownership claim on that underlying portfolio. The token's net asset value (NAV) is either fixed at $1.00 with yield distributed separately (BUIDL, BENJI, USTB, USYC, STBT) or accrued into a growing NAV per share (OUSG, USDY).

The legal structures differ — some are SEC-registered Regulation D 506(c) private placements, some are foreign issuers under Reg S — but functionally, an investor who passes whatever KYC/AML check the issuer requires can deposit USDC (or wire USD) and receive tokens at the prevailing NAV. They can later redeem at the prevailing NAV, again subject to the issuer's process. The yield accrues continuously and is paid out by one of three mechanisms (more on this in the per-token cards below).

Why this is not a stablecoin

USDC is backed by reserves held at custodian banks, but USDC tokens themselves do not represent a security claim — they are e-money. BUIDL, in contrast, is an interest in a fund. That distinction is why every RWA token in our universe has transfer restrictions baked into its smart contract: the transfer() function will revert if the recipient is not on an allowlist. Stablecoins do not need to do that.

Why these seven and not others

The blueprint considered including additional tokens — WTGXX (WisdomTree's tokenized money market fund), TBILL (OpenEden's Treasury fund), and several others. We held the sample at seven for three reasons:

  1. Verification cost. Each token requires a manually-curated issuer wallet exclusion list. Building this for a new token takes several hours of work per token, including reading the issuer's documentation, cross-referencing Etherscan labels, and inspecting suspicious transfer clusters by hand. Seven was the right number for the available time budget.
  2. Sample length. Adding TBILL would have added ~40 weeks of data starting from late 2024, which is too short to identify any treatment effect cleanly. The CS estimator needs enough pre-period observations to estimate $ATT(g,t)$ for $t \ll g$.
  3. Stake in the sample. The seven we picked together cover ~70% of the total tokenized-Treasury market cap. Adding a 20th token that's 0.5% of the market wouldn't meaningfully change the dollar-weighted answer.

Chapter 15 revisits this and discusses what changes if a follow-up paper expands to 15–20 tokens. The short answer: statistical power roughly doubles, the never-treated control group becomes much larger, and several robustness checks that currently lean on a single token (BUIDL's near-zero baseline velocity) become more meaningful.
