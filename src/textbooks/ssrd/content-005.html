The second clause isn't in the SPEC. It means one strong signal plus a shared ASN and shared nameservers alerts. Pick signals below to compare the two rules.

6.3 From pairs to kits: union-find

A kit is built from seed pages, not defined by hand. kits.cluster compares every pair of seed pages on different registrable domains. When a pair is linked, their groups merge. Groups are tracked with a disjoint-set union (union-find): each page points to a parent, find follows pointers to a group's root (halving the path as it goes, so later lookups are fast), and union joins two roots. After all pairs, each root is one candidate kit. Groups spanning ≥ 2 registrable domains become kits, named kit-NN, plus -KCDA if any member is a KCDA seed. Kits whose median page has fewer than 3 investment terms are marked excluded-non-investment and never alert. That catches parking and hosting templates.

6.4 What the library looks like now

The first library (Oct 17) clustered 212 seed pages on 128 domains into 12 kits holding 72 member pages. The largest held 30 pages on 16 domains, all with the same title about "sustainable wealth through oil, real estate, and agriculture". After the copy fingerprint and the chaining fix, the library holds 22 multi-domain kits, including kit-01-KCDA, plus 138 single-site references (ref-<domain>): investment-content seed domains that didn't cluster, so a live page can still match one known listed site. A reference match is weaker evidence than a kit match and is labeled separately. Most kits are general CBR-listed investment kits, not the KCDA network. The README and memo must keep the two apart.

6.5 The backtest: does the library recognize its own kits?

evaluate.backtest holds out about 20% of kit-member pages (page ID mod 5 = 0), re-clusters the rest, and checks whether each held-out page alerts against a group whose majority kit is the page's original kit. The history is a lesson in itself. It went 14 of 17, then 27 of 31 (87%) after the copy fingerprint. Then one 87-page "kit" turned out to be chaining: 4.8% pair density, held together by one inline-script hash (chapter 5.7). After the fix, the honest result is 13 of 19 (68%, 95% CI 46–85%). The 87% was inflated, because held-out pages were easy to "re-identify" inside one giant chained group.

What the backtest does and doesn't show

  • It shows the rule and thresholds recognize known kits from a partial library. It does not measure precision on live CT traffic, where most candidates aren't in any kit.
  • Only pages that joined a kit in the full library are held out. Seed pages the rule never linked to anything are never tested, so 68% is recognition given a page is linkable. The held-out pages are excluded from re-clustering, but the common-value denylist is shared. Report it as recognition, beside live precision.
  • n = 19 is small, hence the wide interval, which reaches below the 60% target. Chapter 8.3 shows why.

Check yourself

A candidate shares TLSH distance 40 and tag-Jaccard 0.91 with a kit member, and nothing else. Alert?
No, under both rules. Both are family A, so there's one strong family and zero other signals. Same template doesn't mean same kit.
Favicon + Telegram handle shared. Alert?
Yes: strong B plus strong C. That's two families.
Why is clustering stricter than alerting?
Union-find is transitive. One loose link merges whole groups, and errors compound across the library. An alert is one pair, and its error stays local and goes to review.
What does the 68% backtest let you claim in the memo?
That the library re-identified 13 of 19 held-out seed pages to the correct kit (95% CI 46–85%), and that an earlier 87% was inflated by chaining. Nothing about live precision, and nothing about who runs the sites.
Why did the inflated 87% look so good?
A chained mega-group contains almost everything, so a held-out page almost always lands back in "its" kit. Big, loose clusters make recognition easy and meaningless. Check cluster density, not just the catch rate.

7.2 urlscan: search and fetch, never submit

urlscan.io is a public archive of browser scans other people submitted. SSRD uses it for history: what a seed domain looked like before it was seized or went dark. The free tier allowed 1,000 searches, 10,000 result fetches and some scans per day; the real limits come from GET /user/quotas/. Search sees only public scans, and anonymous result fetches return 403, so the API key in data/.env is required. The searches SSRD actually runs look like this:

urlscan search syntax (Elasticsearch query strings)

  • page.title:"This Website Has Been Seized": every scan with that page title (finds seizure banners, many of them not KCDA).
  • hash:<sha256>: every scan where some response body had this SHA-256. This is how you pivot from one known script or banner to other domains.
  • page.domain:"example.com": the history of one seed domain.
  • Also free: page.asn, ip. Pro only: similar-page search, text.content, dom.hash. There's no mmh3 favicon hash and no TLSH, so SSRD computes those itself.

Hard rule 4 says never write code that submits a scan, in any visibility. A public or unlisted scan publishes the domain, and might warn the operator or expose an investigation. A private scan is urlscan's browser actively visiting the site with JS on, which goes beyond the passive-visit rule. A test enforces that the client has no submission code path. The ToS also say personal, non-commercial use and no "systematic retrieval" without permission. So: email urlscan about the research use before bulk pulls, keep to min_interval_seconds = 2.0, back off on 429, and cache every response locally.
