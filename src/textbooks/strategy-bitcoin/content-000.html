Understanding Strategy, Bitcoin, and MSTR

The central idea: Strategy turns access to capital markets into Bitcoin exposure. Understanding the company requires understanding both Bitcoin and the claims attached to the money used to acquire it.

This guide connects all seven resources previously recommended. How to use this guide

Read in order the first time. Estimated study time is 90–120 minutes, excluding the original books and full podcast episodes. The estimate is a planning suggestion, not measured reading time.

  1. Foundations: explain money, Bitcoin, and the corporate wrapper.
  2. Financing: follow a dollar from an investor into the treasury and identify the claim it creates.
  3. Measurement: calculate per-share exposure, valuation, income, and downside.
  4. Evidence: revisit the original sources with specific questions.
  5. Review: answer without looking, then compare with the answer key.

The worked examples are invented illustrations. They are deliberately small and exclude taxes, fees, and other real-world complications unless stated. They are not price forecasts or estimates of Strategy’s fair value.

1. Three propositions you must evaluate separately

Bitcoin proposition: Bitcoin’s monetary and settlement properties could create lasting demand.

Company proposition: Strategy could finance and manage a Bitcoin treasury in ways that benefit particular investors.

Security proposition: A particular Strategy security could offer attractive economics at the price paid and under its actual terms.

The first proposition does not prove the second or third. You can believe Bitcoin will become more valuable while believing that MSTR’s price already assumes too much success. You can prefer one preferred stock’s payment rights while declining common-stock exposure. These are different questions.

2. What money does, and why the books matter

Money helps people quote prices, exchange goods, and transfer purchasing power across time. These functions can pull in different directions. A currency can be convenient for payments while losing purchasing power over many years.

Lyn Alden’s lens: the technology used to move information and settle value shapes monetary institutions. Broken Money connects monetary history, banking, and the possible future of digital money. Its author’s public overview frames the problem globally, including countries with fragile currencies. Treat this as the book’s argument, not an established prediction about Bitcoin. Broken Money author overview

Saifedean Ammous’s lens: monetary goods differ in how easily their supply expands. The Bitcoin Standard argues that money resistant to supply expansion supports saving and long-term planning. Its publisher presents Bitcoin as a potential digital successor to gold. This is an explicitly pro-Bitcoin, Austrian-economics perspective. Book and official sample

Your checkpoint: supply discipline is one property of money. It does not establish future demand, price stability, widespread adoption, or a fair purchase price.

Distinguish three ideas often blended in interviews:

  • Consumer-price inflation: a change in the cost of a measured basket of goods and services.
  • Money-supply growth: a change in a particular measure of money. Which measure and period matter.
  • Asset-price appreciation: a change in the market price of houses, shares, Bitcoin, or other assets.

They interact, but one percentage cannot be substituted for another. When someone says money is losing value, ask: against what basket, asset, currency, or time period?

3. Bitcoin’s mechanism, before the investment story

Bitcoin is a network and an asset recorded on that network. Cryptographic signatures authorize spending. Nodes check transactions against shared rules. Miners expend computing resources to propose blocks through proof of work. The chain makes conflicting payment histories costly to establish. Confirmation reduces reversal risk; it is not an unconditional guarantee under every attack model. Original white paper, sections 2–6 and 11

Bitcoin’s issuance rules converge toward roughly 21 million coins. A satoshi is one hundred-millionth of a bitcoin. The supply limit depends on the rules participants enforce, rather than a company promising to limit issuance. A majority of miners cannot simply force existing validating nodes to accept an incompatible supply rule. Bitcoin.org FAQ, Blockstream’s explanation of nodes and consensus

Keep the distinctions straight:

  • Scarcity: the supply of valid coins is constrained under the accepted rules.
  • Security: changing transaction history is costly under particular network assumptions.
  • Demand: people choose to hold, use, or accept the asset.
  • Price: the exchange rate at which buyers and sellers currently trade it.

Scarcity without demand does not guarantee value. Mining cost does not set a guaranteed price floor. Bitcoin itself does not promise a coupon or corporate dividend; selling, lending, or packaging it introduces separate economics and risks. Bitcoin.org FAQ

4. Saylor’s thesis, translated into testable claims

The Lex Fridman conversation explains Saylor’s reasons for favoring a scarce, portable digital asset over holding large cash balances. His comparisons with property, energy, and digitized information explain his worldview. They do not establish a valuation formula or a required future return. The episode predates today’s preferred-stock complex. Lex #276 transcript

Read a phrase such as “digital energy” as a metaphor for storing and moving economic value. Bitcoin is not a battery that returns the electricity spent mining it. Ask what the metaphor helps explain and what it leaves out.

Translate advocacy into questions:

  1. What sustains demand for Bitcoin over the holding period?
  2. Why should this asset outperform the alternatives after risk and costs?
  3. What drawdown can the owner survive without needing to sell?
  4. What observation would weaken the thesis?

These questions turn a persuasive narrative into something you can examine.

5. What Strategy actually owns and does

Strategy is a corporation with a Bitcoin treasury and an enterprise analytics software business. It uses equity and debt financing, alongside operating cash flows, to support its treasury strategy. Michael Saylor is executive chairman; Phong Le is president and CEO. Strategy Investor Relations

The software business sells analytics capabilities to customers. The treasury activity acquires and manages assets and financing. Software revenue, operating cash, capital raised, and changes in Bitcoin’s market value answer different questions. Do not treat them as interchangeable forms of earnings.

A dated anchor: the October 19, 2026 filing reports 848,517 BTC held as of October 18. It reports a $4.83 billion USD Reserve and $447.3 million of separate USD Cash. During that week, it used USD Cash for STRC repurchases and reserve cash for preferred dividends. These balances are observations at that date, not permanent resources. October 19 8-K, pages 2–3

Important update: the July 2026 presentation describes active capital management, including Bitcoin sales, USD reserves, and security repurchases. “It only buys Bitcoin and will never sell” is therefore an inadequate description of the reviewed strategy. The same presentation distinguishes its reserve policy from a contractual requirement to maintain that reserve. Q2 2026 presentation, slides 19–29

6. Owning Bitcoin and owning MSTR are different exposures

Direct Bitcoin: you hold the asset, with custody, transaction, and market risks. You have no claim on Strategy.

Spot Bitcoin exchange-traded product: you hold a security designed to provide exposure to Bitcoin held by that vehicle. Custody, fees, tracking, trading hours, and redemption arrangements depend on its prospectus. Retail investors should not assume they can redeem shares for coins.

MSTR common stock: you hold an ownership interest in Strategy. Its assets, debt, preferred stock, operating business, management decisions, share issuance, and market valuation all matter. It is a residual claim: other claims must be satisfied ahead of common shareholders.

Strategy preferred stock: you hold a particular class of corporate equity with specified payment and liquidation rights. You do not receive unlimited Bitcoin upside merely because the company owns Bitcoin.

Neither common nor preferred stock gives you a direct redemption right to the company’s Bitcoin. Strategy dashboard notes

Checkpoint: explain why “Bitcoin rose 20%, so MSTR must rise 20%” is unsupported before moving on.

Follow the financing

7. The capital-market loop

Start with the actual exchange: investors provide dollars and receive a security. Strategy can use proceeds to acquire Bitcoin or for other permitted corporate purposes. Each financing changes the assets and the claims on those assets.

In a favorable loop, investor demand supports useful issuance terms; issuance increases the treasury; expected future execution supports demand for the securities. In an unfavorable loop, falling asset prices and a shrinking valuation premium can make further financing less attractive. Neither loop is a law of nature. VanEck’s May 22, 2025 analysis

“Raising money” does not automatically mean creating value. Always ask who supplied it, what they received, and who bears the downside.

8. Common-stock issuance: a smaller percentage can own more exposure

An at-the-market offering, or ATM, allows a company to sell newly issued securities through agents into the market under its offering program. Available program capacity is permission to issue; it is not cash already raised. Strategy’s offering documents describe its programs and their risks. Base prospectus supplement

Illustration A — no debt, no preferred stock, no other assets or costs.

The company holds 100 BTC and has 100 shares. Bitcoin costs $100 per coin. Each share corresponds to 1 BTC of gross holdings.

  • Issue 10 new shares at $200 each: raise $2,000.
  • Buy 20 BTC: holdings become 120 BTC.
  • Shares become 110: gross exposure becomes 120 ÷ 110 = 1.0909 BTC per share.

The old owner’s percentage of the company falls, but BTC per share rises 9.09%. Share-count dilution and asset-per-share accretion can happen together.

Change the issue price to $50. Ten shares raise $500 and acquire 5 BTC. Exposure becomes 105 ÷ 110 = 0.9545 BTC per share, a 4.55% decline.

The gross-accretion condition is simple under these assumptions: net issue proceeds per new share must buy more Bitcoin than existing gross BTC per share. Fees lower the proceeds. Spending some money on dividends rather than Bitcoin changes the result. Senior claims require a separate net calculation.

Your question: accretive to which metric—gross BTC, net assets, earnings, or something else? The word is incomplete without that answer.

9. Convertible debt: low cash interest has a price

A convertible bond combines a debt claim with a contractual option to receive common-stock exposure under specified conditions. A conversion price helps determine the shares associated with that option. Its value depends on price, volatility, time, and contract terms.

Illustration B: a $1,000 bond converts into 10 shares, implying a $100 conversion price. If the shares are worth $150, the shares represented by conversion are worth $1,500. If shares are worth $50, that conversion value is $500; the debt claim still matters. Actual conversion eligibility and cash/share settlement follow the contract.

An investor may accept a lower cash coupon because the embedded option is valuable. Some buyers hedge common-stock exposure rather than making an unhedged bullish bet. The issuer’s economic cost includes potential dilution and contractual obligations. A 0% coupon is not free capital. VanEck, convertible-financing discussion

Read these dates separately: maturity, holder put, and issuer call. Maturity is the scheduled repayment date. A put can allow a holder to require earlier repurchase under stated conditions. A call allows the issuer to redeem under stated conditions. A debt schedule that lists only final maturities can miss earlier cash requirements. Q2 presentation, slide 56 and its duration footnote
