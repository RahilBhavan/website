Build scenarios before calculating a terminal value

A discounted cash-flow model is only applicable to the specific cash flows the holder can receive. An analyst cannot make a governance token into equity by placing protocol fees into a spreadsheet. Establish the cash-flow right first.

Suppose an instrument is entitled to $1 million at each year-end for three years and no later payment. At 10%, present value is approximately $2.487 million. Changing the discount rate changes value. Changing the legal entitlement can remove the entire premise. A terminal-value assumption can dominate a model, so show finite-horizon results separately.

Build at least three scenarios with explicit drivers. For a service, separate customer count, usage per customer, price, supplier share, expenses, dilution and holder distribution. Do not independently choose incompatible assumptions. Very low prices may increase demand but reduce margin; high token prices may raise collateral capacity but increase the dollar cost of token-denominated rewards.

Try it: forecast one year of holder cash flow under a 50% revenue decline and fixed operating costs. Then identify which model inputs are actual observations and which are your assumptions. An uncertainty range is more informative than a long decimal answer built from guesses.

Real-world assets

What do you own, where does the cash come from, and what happens in a default?

Outcome and entry test

You will trace a digital instrument to its underlying asset, obligations, cash flows, legal rights and failure procedure. Start with the asset and the claim

After this chapter: Trace a token to an exact legal claim and asset owner.

Watch for this mistake: A bond-backed token means each holder directly owns the individual bonds.

Separate the reference asset from the instrument you hold. A token might be a direct registered fund share, a debt claim against an issuer, a beneficial interest through a vehicle, a custodial receipt or a derivative tracking a price. Similar tickers can conceal different counterparties and remedies.

A special-purpose vehicle (SPV) is a legal entity created for a defined purpose. It can separate activities, but the label alone does not establish bankruptcy remoteness. Documents must establish asset ownership, limited obligations, segregation, security interests where applicable and the investor's position if a party fails. Legal effectiveness depends on jurisdiction and structure.

Worked chain: investor -> tokenized fund share -> fund -> custodian -> Treasury bills. The investor may own a share of the fund, not an individually identified government bill. A different structure could be investor -> issuer IOU -> issuer portfolio. The assets could be similar while investor exposure differs substantially.

Create a rights sheet: issuer; governing documents; applicable law; register of record; legal holder; asset owner; custodian; cash account; transfer restrictions; redemption counterparty; insolvency ranking; disputes; administrator powers. Put "unverified" in any blank rather than filling it with marketing language.

Fresh check R1: a token tracks gold's price but its terms give only an unsecured claim against a company and no delivery right. Explain why owning it differs from owning an allocated bar. Name the documents that could settle the distinction.

Identify asset ownership and segregation, each holder's insolvency ranking, custody and liabilities, plus the enforceable redemption and recovery terms. Similar reference assets do not establish identical rights.

Distinguish an issuer IOU from a registered fund share.

An IOU is a claim against its issuer under its terms. A fund share is an interest in a vehicle under its documents. Neither label alone grants personal title to each portfolio asset.

Loop 02 / Chapter 08

Understand the underlying economics

After this chapter: Model the underlying asset's cash flows before analyzing its wrapper.

Watch for this mistake: Rent or coupon receipts equal money distributable to investors.

The chain does not remove the asset's ordinary risks. A government bill has currency, issuer and market risks. A loan has borrower, collateral and recovery risk. A building has occupancy, maintenance, insurance, leverage and sale risks. Gold has storage, authenticity, custody and price risks; it does not inherently generate interest income.

A bond pays according to specified terms. Its price can change as required yields change. For a short educational example, a zero-coupon payment of $1,000 in one year is worth $952.38 at 5% and $925.93 at 8%. Tokenizing it does not fix that price. Duration approximates interest-rate sensitivity locally: percentage price change is approximately -modified duration times yield change in decimal terms, holding other factors constant. Large changes need better models.

For loans, expected credit loss is often summarized as PD x LGD x EAD: probability of default, loss given default and exposure at default over a defined horizon. This is a simplified expected-loss model; correlations, timing and changing exposure matter. A 4% PD, 60% LGD and $1 million EAD gives $24,000 expected loss. It says little about worst-case losses or the date cash will return.

Worked property example: annual rent $240,000, operating expenses $90,000, interest $70,000, capital reserve $30,000. Simplified cash before tax and principal repayment is $50,000. Dividing rent by token purchase price overstates distributable income. If rent falls $40,000 while other costs remain, the residual falls 80%.

Fresh check R2: a property receives $300,000 rent, spends $120,000 on operations, $100,000 on interest and $40,000 on reserves. Calculate residual cash. Then reduce rent 10%. Explain why the percentage fall in residual exceeds the fall in rent.

Which obligations can sit between gross asset income and holder distributions?

Operations, servicing, fees, interest, principal repayment, taxes and reserves, depending on the actual structure.

Loop 02 / Chapter 09

NAV, yield and valuation uncertainty

After this chapter: Calculate NAV and test whether a quoted discount is executable.

Watch for this mistake: A market price below NAV is guaranteed arbitrage.

Net asset value (NAV) = fair or stipulated asset value minus liabilities under the product's valuation policy. NAV per unit divides that value by outstanding eligible units. Market price can differ from NAV when transfer restrictions, redemption frictions, stale valuations or investor sentiment matter.

Worked example: assets $10.2 million, liabilities $200,000, 10 million units. NAV is $1 per unit. A $0.98 market quote is a 2% discount. It is not automatically free money: can you buy, redeem, satisfy minimums, pay fees and receive cash in time? Is NAV fresh and recoverable? Arbitrage requires an executable path, not just two displayed numbers.

Distributions are cash paid out. Accrued yield is income recognized over time. Total return combines price change and distributions with clear reinvestment assumptions. Some instruments keep a roughly constant unit price and distribute income; others accumulate income through a changing unit value or balance. A cash distribution can include a return of capital, so cash paid is not automatically economic profit.

A reserve attestation, financial-statement audit, asset valuation and smart-contract audit answer different questions. Scope, date, assurance standard and exclusions matter. A wallet balance can establish that an address holds a token; it does not establish that the issuer has no hidden liabilities or that collateral is unencumbered.

Fresh check R3: assets $5.5 million, liabilities $500,000, 4 million units. Find NAV per unit. At a market price of $1.20, calculate the discount. List three reasons a trader may fail to realize the apparent difference.
