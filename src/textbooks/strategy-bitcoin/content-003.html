30. Answer key

  1. The asset can have demand; the company can create useful exposure; the specific security can offer worthwhile economics at the price paid. None automatically proves the next.
  2. Supply is only one side of the market. Demand, alternatives, regulation, liquidity, and holder behavior also affect price.
  3. An intermediary can update an account or transmit instructions before the underlying settlement asset actually transfers. Identify which claim changed.
  4. Strategy owns it. A shareholder owns corporate equity, with no direct right to redeem that equity for the treasury’s Bitcoin.
  5. It raises $2,000 and buys 20 BTC. 120 ÷ 110 = 1.0909 BTC per share, up 9.09%. Ownership percentage falls while gross exposure per share rises.
  6. It raises $500 and buys 5 BTC. 105 ÷ 110 = 0.9545 BTC per share, down 4.55%.
  7. The conversion option can have value. The issuer accepts potential dilution and debt obligations; a low cash coupon is not zero economic cost.
  8. The asset addition comes with a senior claim. Raising $1,000 at $1,000 notional and buying assets leaves the simplified common residual unchanged initially, before costs and future distributions.
  9. Cumulative unpaid amounts accumulate under the contract. Undeclared non-cumulative dividends do not create the same accumulated entitlement. Neither structure alone guarantees punctual payment.
  10. $10 ÷ $80 = 12.5%. Price changes, skipped payments, redemption, currency, taxes, and fees affect realized return.
  11. $200 becomes $120: −40%, before any dividends or costs.
  12. The residual falls from $70 to $20: −71.43%. This is a simplified balance-sheet calculation, not a traded-price prediction.
  13. The ratio may omit principal, put dates, operating cash needs, or other uses of the reserve. Timing and usable cash matter.
  14. Follow each metric’s definition. Gross BPS uses Strategy’s assumed diluted count; current Net BPS uses its specified fully diluted count and claim treatment. Do not mix denominators or double-count conversion.
  15. Read the approval, filing, effective date, declaration, record date, and operational conditions. A proposal or target date is insufficient.

31. Three transfer exercises

These require applying the mechanism to a new case, rather than repeating a definition.

Exercise A — choose the denominator

A hypothetical company holds 50 BTC, worth $100 each, plus $500 cash. It has $1,000 non-convertible debt, $500 non-convertible preferred claims, and 100 common shares. Ignore every other asset and claim. Shares trade at $60.

Calculate gross Bitcoin dollars per share, simplified net value per share, and the market multiple of that net value. Explain why the gross ratio and net multiple differ.

Answer: gross is $5,000 ÷ 100 = $50. Net is ($5,000 + $500 − $1,000 − $500) ÷ 100 = $40. The net multiple is $60 ÷ $40 = 1.5×, or a 50% premium. Price divided by gross Bitcoin dollars gives 1.2×. Those ratios use different denominators; neither is an exact reproduction of Strategy’s full metric.

Exercise B — trace a cash deadline

A hypothetical issuer has $300 usable cash, no operating cash inflow, $60 annual cash dividends, $10 interest, and a $250 principal payment due within twelve months. Assume no new financing or asset sales.

Answer: the narrow dividend/interest coverage is $300 ÷ $70 = 4.29 years. Twelve-month uses are $320, creating a $20 shortfall. A multi-year coverage headline misses the principal deadline. The issuer would need a different action; the problem does not itself establish which action it could take.

Exercise C — challenge a headline

Someone says: “Bitcoin holdings grew 20%, so common shareholders became 20% richer.”

Answer: request the starting and ending diluted shares, asset prices, cash, senior claims, financing uses, and common-stock valuation. If holdings grew 20% while shares grew 30%, gross BTC per share changed by 1.20 ÷ 1.30 − 1 = −7.69%. Even positive gross accretion would not settle net value or stock return.

32. Explain it in five sentences

Try writing your own explanation before reading this model:

  1. Strategy owns a Bitcoin treasury alongside an analytics software business.
  2. It finances assets and corporate needs using common equity, preferred equity, debt, cash, and capital-management actions.
  3. Each financing gives investors different claims, so more Bitcoin does not automatically mean more value for each common share.
  4. MSTR returns depend on the residual assets, share count, financing costs, and the market’s valuation of future execution.
  5. Understanding the risk requires checking both asset value and cash deadlines under unfavorable conditions.

Change the numbers

A 50% Bitcoin decline can reduce a $70 residual to $20.

Illustration only. Starting Bitcoin assets are $100. You choose the senior claims; they remain fixed within each scenario. The initial share-price proxy is 1.5 times the initial residual. There is no cash, software value, conversion, issuance, tax, cost, dividend, or timing effect. This is not a model of MSTR’s actual price.

Bitcoin assets: $100 → $50. Fixed senior claims: $30.

Residual for common: $70 → $20 (−71.43%).

Illustrative price proxy: $105 → $20 (−80.95%).

The residual changes because fixed claims absorb a larger share of a smaller asset pool. The price proxy changes again when the market multiple changes. Neither is a default probability.

At or below a zero residual, the toy model shows common equity as zero. Real companies can trade above a simple residual because of optionality, operations, or future actions; legal recovery follows actual contracts and law.
