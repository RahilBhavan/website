Chapter 07 · Part III — Causal Inference

Causal inference primer

Potential outcomes, the fundamental problem, average treatment effects, and the difference-in-differences identification strategy in its simplest 2×2 form.

The Rubin potential-outcomes framework

Imagine OUSG in week 50 of our sample. Two parallel worlds exist:

  • $Y_{\text{OUSG}, 50}(1)$ — OUSG's velocity in week 50 in the world where it was AMM-listed. (This is the world we observe.)
  • $Y_{\text{OUSG}, 50}(0)$ — OUSG's velocity in week 50 in the counterfactual world where it was not AMM-listed. (This is the world we cannot observe.)

The causal effect of AMM listing on OUSG in week 50 is the difference between these two potential outcomes:

$$\tau_{\text{OUSG}, 50} = Y_{\text{OUSG}, 50}(1) - Y_{\text{OUSG}, 50}(0)$$

This quantity is the holy grail. It is also fundamentally unobservable, because we only ever see one of the two potential outcomes for any given unit at any given time. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference (Holland 1986). Every causal estimator is, at heart, a strategy for plausibly imputing the missing potential outcome.

The first estimand: ATT, the average treatment effect on the treated

Because we cannot estimate individual treatment effects, we settle for averages. The Average Treatment effect on the Treated (ATT) is the average of $\tau_{i,t}$ across the units and time periods where treatment was actually applied:

$$\text{ATT} = \mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(1) - Y_{i,t}(0) \mid D_{i,t} = 1]$$

where $D_{i,t} = 1$ indicates that unit $i$ was treated in period $t$. ATT answers the question "for the tokens that were AMM-listed during our sample, how much did listing change their average velocity?" Note it does not answer "what would happen if we listed BUIDL on an AMM" — that's a different estimand (ATU, average treatment effect on the untreated) and harder to identify.

The Callaway-Sant'Anna estimator estimates a richer object — $\text{ATT}(g, t)$, the ATT for treatment cohort $g$ in calendar period $t$ — but every aggregation of CS estimates rolls back into an ATT-style summary. Keep this in mind: throughout the paper, when we say "the treatment effect", we strictly mean ATT, never ATE.

The simplest identification strategy: 2×2 difference-in-differences

Suppose, for a moment, we had only one treated token (OUSG) and one untreated token (BUIDL), and two periods: a "before" period (averaged over weeks before OUSG's listing) and an "after" period (averaged over weeks after). We observe:

BeforeAfter
OUSG (treated)$\bar Y_{\text{O, pre}}$$\bar Y_{\text{O, post}}$
BUIDL (control)$\bar Y_{\text{B, pre}}$$\bar Y_{\text{B, post}}$

The 2×2 DiD estimator of the ATT for OUSG is the "difference of differences":

$$\widehat{\text{ATT}}_{\text{DiD}} = \left( \bar Y_{\text{O, post}} - \bar Y_{\text{O, pre}} \right) - \left( \bar Y_{\text{B, post}} - \bar Y_{\text{B, pre}} \right)$$

In words: the change in OUSG's velocity around its listing event, minus the change in BUIDL's velocity over the same calendar period. The intuition is that BUIDL's change tells us how much OUSG would have changed even without listing, due to common time effects (macro, sector, weather, anything). Netting that out leaves us with the part of OUSG's change that is specific to its treatment.

The identifying assumption: parallel trends

The 2×2 DiD estimator is unbiased for the ATT if and only if the counterfactual outcome trajectory for the treated unit (had it not been treated) would have moved in parallel with the control unit's actual trajectory:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_{\text{O,post}}(0) - Y_{\text{O,pre}}(0)\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_{\text{B,post}}(0) - Y_{\text{B,pre}}(0)\right]$$

This is the parallel trends assumption. It is not testable directly (we cannot observe $Y_{\text{O,post}}(0)$ — that's the whole point), but we can look at pre-treatment trends as a plausibility check. If OUSG and BUIDL moved in parallel for many weeks before OUSG's listing, the assumption is more plausible than if they were diverging. (More on the limits of this check in Chapter 12 — it is much less informative than it appears.)

From 2×2 to two-way fixed effects

With more than two units or more than two periods, the natural generalization of DiD is a panel regression with token fixed effects and time fixed effects:

$$Y_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \delta_t + \beta \cdot D_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}$$

Here $\alpha_i$ is a fixed effect for each token (absorbing time-invariant differences across tokens — BUIDL's institutional KYC, BENJI's chain), $\delta_t$ is a fixed effect for each week (absorbing common shocks — FOMC, regulatory news), and $D_{i,t}$ is a binary indicator that equals 1 once token $i$ has been treated. The coefficient $\beta$ is — under the parallel trends assumption — the average treatment effect.

This is called the two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression, and it has been the workhorse of empirical economics for forty years. It is ALMOST always what someone writes down first when they have panel data with staggered treatment.

And — this is where Chapter 8 picks up — it turns out that when treatment is staggered across units (different units treated in different periods, as in our project), the TWFE coefficient $\beta$ is NOT generally a clean estimate of the ATT. It is a weighted average of many 2×2 comparisons. The weights themselves are non-negative and sum to 1, but a subset of those comparisons silently use already-treated units as controls — and under dynamic treatment effects, those comparisons subtract a chunk of the treatment effect from the implied ATT estimate. This is the Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition, and understanding it is the gateway to understanding why we use Callaway-Sant'Anna instead.

Recap before the next chapter

Three things to lock in:

  • Causal effects live in counterfactual world. We never observe both potential outcomes.
  • DiD identifies ATT under the parallel-trends assumption. Plausibility is supported (not proven) by pre-treatment parallel motion.
  • TWFE extends DiD to large panels but breaks down under staggered treatment, in a way you have to understand precisely.
Chapter 08 · Part III — Causal Inference

TWFE and the Goodman-Bacon decomposition

Why the workhorse two-way fixed-effects estimator gives the wrong answer under staggered treatment, not because its weights are negative (they aren't) but because some of the implicit 2×2 comparisons it averages use already-treated units as controls.

The setup

Chapter 7 ended with the TWFE specification:

$$Y_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \delta_t + \beta \cdot D_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}$$

Three treated cohorts in our project: OUSG at week 44, USDY at week 68, STBT at week 92. Four never-treated controls. A naive reader might assume the TWFE coefficient $\hat\beta$ is some kind of weighted average of the individual treatment effects for these three cohorts.

The Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition tells us exactly what $\hat\beta$ is: it is a weighted average of every possible 2×2 DiD comparison hidden inside the panel. And — this is the surprise — some of those comparisons use already-treated units as controls for not-yet-treated units. When the treatment effect grows over time (a "dynamic treatment effect", which is exactly what we expect for AMM listing), those comparisons enter the weighted average with the wrong sign and bias $\hat\beta$ downward — possibly all the way through zero.
